# Reading Parcel: The Widening and Narrowing of the Vote, 1865 to Today

*Prepared at your request, November 3, 2026. You asked how the franchise was widened after 1865, through which amendments and struggles, and where it was narrowed again. This parcel tells you what happened and where people disagree. It does not tell you what to think. Where Americans still argue, both sides are given in their own strongest terms.*

---

## A word before beginning

You left off in April 1865. The Thirteenth Amendment had passed Congress and was going to the states. It was ratified in December 1865 and abolished slavery everywhere in the Union. In your last public address, on April 11, 1865, you said you would prefer that the vote be given to "the very intelligent" among the freedmen and "those who serve our cause as soldiers." That was the first time a President had publicly favored any Black suffrage. What came after went much further than that, then fell back a long way, and then went forward again.

One thing to keep in mind throughout: the original Constitution left the question of who may vote mostly to the states. Nearly every change since then has come either from an amendment saying what a state may *not* use to deny the vote, or from Congress and the courts deciding how far federal power reaches to enforce those amendments. Most of the arguments, then and now, turn on that boundary.

---

## I. Reconstruction and the War Amendments (1865–1870)

### President Johnson and the Black Codes

Andrew Johnson became President on your death. He offered generous pardons to former Confederates and let the Southern states rebuild their governments quickly, without any requirement of Black suffrage. In 1865 and 1866 those governments passed "Black Codes." These laws restricted where freedmen could work, travel, and own property, and punished "vagrancy" with forced labor. Many Northerners concluded that the South was trying to restore slavery in another form. Congress refused to seat the Southern delegations, and a long fight between the President and Congress began. It ended with Johnson's impeachment in 1868. The Senate acquitted him by one vote.

### The Fourteenth Amendment (ratified 1868)

Congress, under the Republican majority, drafted the Fourteenth Amendment. Its main clauses:

- **Citizenship:** All persons born or naturalized in the United States, and subject to its jurisdiction, are citizens. This overturned the *Dred Scott* decision of 1857.
- **Due process and equal protection:** No state may deprive any person of life, liberty, or property without due process of law, nor deny any person the equal protection of the laws. In the twentieth century these became the most-cited words in the Constitution.
- **Section 2, representation:** The three-fifths rule ended. But if a state denied the vote to any *male* inhabitants aged twenty-one and up, its representation in Congress would be cut in proportion. This was the first time the word "male" appeared in the Constitution. Women's suffrage advocates, many of them abolitionists, felt betrayed by it. Section 2's penalty has never actually been applied.
- **Section 3:** Former officials who had sworn to support the Constitution and then joined the rebellion were barred from office unless Congress removed the bar by a two-thirds vote. Congress largely removed it in 1872.

Southern states were required to ratify the amendment before being readmitted. Under the Reconstruction Acts of 1867, the army oversaw new state constitutional conventions, and Black men voted in them.

### The Fifteenth Amendment (ratified 1870)

"The right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude." Congress was given power to enforce it.

Notice what it does *not* say. It does not grant a right to vote. It forbids one ground of denial. Its framers debated a broader version that would have also banned denial for lack of property or education. They rejected it, partly because Northern and Western states used such tests themselves, against Irish and Chinese immigrants among others. That narrower wording left room for the methods described in Part III.

Ulysses S. Grant was elected President in 1868 and supported the amendment. He called its ratification "a measure of grander importance than any other one act of the kind from the foundation of our free Government."

### Black voting and officeholding

The change was immense. In the late 1860s and the 1870s, Black men voted in large numbers across the South. Often they were majorities of the electorate in South Carolina, Mississippi, and Louisiana. Historians count more than 1,500 Black officeholders in the Reconstruction South, among them:

- **Hiram Revels** of Mississippi, the first Black United States Senator (1870). He was seated in the very seat Jefferson Davis had left.
- **Blanche K. Bruce** of Mississippi, the first Black senator to serve a full term (1875–1881).
- **Joseph Rainey** of South Carolina, the first Black member of the House (1870).
- **P. B. S. Pinchback**, briefly acting governor of Louisiana (1872–73).
- Hundreds of state legislators, sheriffs, justices of the peace, and school officials.

The Reconstruction legislatures founded the South's first public school systems for Black and white children, and also built railroads and courthouses. Their record has been fought over by historians ever since. An older school, associated with William Dunning of Columbia University in the early 1900s, described these governments as corrupt and incompetent. Later historians, notably W. E. B. Du Bois (*Black Reconstruction*, 1935) and Eric Foner (*Reconstruction*, 1988), argued that the corruption was no worse than in Northern cities of the same years. They called these governments a real, if short-lived, experiment in interracial democracy. Most historians today hold something near the second view, though they still argue over the governments' finances and their mistakes.

### Violence and the Enforcement Acts

From the start, the new order was resisted with violence. The Ku Klux Klan, founded in Tennessee in 1865–66, and later groups such as the White League and the Red Shirts attacked Black voters and their white Republican allies. In the Colfax Massacre in Louisiana, on Easter Sunday 1873, dozens of Black men were killed, perhaps as many as 150. Congress passed the Enforcement Acts and the Ku Klux Klan Act (1870–71), and Grant used federal troops and suspended habeas corpus in nine South Carolina counties. For a time the Klan was broken.

The Supreme Court then narrowed those laws. In *United States v. Cruikshank* (1876), arising from Colfax, it held that the Fourteenth Amendment restrained states, not private individuals, and the convictions were thrown out. In *United States v. Reese* (1876) it struck down parts of the Enforcement Act as written too broadly.

## II. Redemption and the End of Reconstruction (1873–1877)

The Southern Democrats who regained control called it "Redemption." A financial panic in 1873, Northern weariness, and scandals in Grant's administration all weakened Northern will to keep at it. One by one, Southern state governments returned to Democratic control, often after elections marked by intimidation. Mississippi in 1875 was the plainest case: the "Mississippi Plan" of armed clubs and open threats.

The presidential election of 1876 between Rutherford B. Hayes (Republican) and Samuel Tilden (Democrat) was disputed in three Southern states. An electoral commission gave every disputed vote to Hayes. By an informal understanding, often called the Compromise of 1877 (historians argue over how much was actually agreed), Hayes withdrew the last federal troops guarding the statehouses in South Carolina and Louisiana. Reconstruction is generally dated as ending there.

## III. Disfranchisement and Jim Crow (1880s–1940s)

Black men still voted in considerable numbers for some years after 1877. In the 1880s and 1890s some Black and poor white farmers even joined in Populist and "Readjuster" coalitions. In response, from 1890 to about 1908, every former Confederate state rewrote its constitution or laws to shut most Black voters out while staying technically within the wording of the Fifteenth Amendment. The Mississippi Constitution of 1890 led the way. A delegate to Alabama's 1901 convention stated its aim openly as establishing "white supremacy in this State," and the convention's president used the same phrase.

The principal methods:

- **Poll taxes:** A yearly fee to vote, often cumulative, so that unpaid back years had to be paid too. It fell heavily on the poor of both races.
- **Literacy and "understanding" tests:** The voter had to read or explain a passage of the state constitution to the satisfaction of a local registrar. Registrars had wide discretion and used it against Black applicants, some of whom were college graduates.
- **Grandfather clauses:** These exempted from the tests anyone whose grandfather had been able to vote before 1867, which in practice meant whites. The Supreme Court struck them down in *Guinn v. United States* (1915).
- **White primaries:** In a one-party South, the Democratic primary was the only election that counted. The party declared itself a private club open to whites only. The Court ended this in *Smith v. Allwright* (1944).
- **Residency rules, complex registration, and criminal-disfranchisement laws** written to target offenses thought to be common among Black men.
- **Violence and economic reprisal:** Lynching, loss of employment, and eviction for those who tried.

The results were stark. In Louisiana, registered Black voters fell from about 130,000 in 1896 to about 1,300 by 1904. In *Williams v. Mississippi* (1898) the Supreme Court upheld Mississippi's scheme because its text did not mention race. In *Giles v. Harris* (1903) it declined to intervene in Alabama. *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896) upheld "separate but equal" segregation more generally. From 1901, when George H. White of North Carolina left the House, until 1929, no Black man sat in Congress.

Some poor and illiterate whites were also disfranchised. Historians disagree about how far this was an unintended effect and how far it was welcomed by the planters and town elites who wrote the laws.

## IV. Women's Suffrage (1848–1920 and after)

Women had petitioned for the vote since the Seneca Falls Convention of 1848. After the war the movement split over the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments. Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Susan B. Anthony opposed ratifying an amendment that enfranchised Black men but left out all women, and at times they used ugly racial arguments in doing so. Lucy Stone, Frederick Douglass, and others supported it as the possible step of that hour. Douglass said that for Black men the vote was a matter of life and death in a way it was not yet for women. The two wings reunited in 1890 as the National American Woman Suffrage Association.

The "New Departure" argument held that women, being citizens under the Fourteenth Amendment, already had the vote. Anthony voted in Rochester in 1872 and was arrested and convicted. In *Minor v. Happersett* (1875) the Supreme Court ruled unanimously that citizenship did not carry a right to vote.

Progress came state by state, mostly in the West. Wyoming Territory gave women the vote in 1869 and insisted on keeping it at statehood in 1890. Utah, Colorado, Idaho, and by 1918 New York and others followed. Opponents, women among them, argued that suffrage would draw women out of the home, that women were already represented through their families, or that it was a matter for the states. In the 1910s Alice Paul's National Woman's Party picketed the White House. Its members were jailed, went on hunger strike, and were force-fed, while the larger association under Carrie Chapman Catt lobbied state by state. Women's war work in 1917–18 helped turn President Woodrow Wilson.

**The Nineteenth Amendment** (ratified August 1920): "The right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged … on account of sex." Tennessee was the deciding state, by one vote in its lower house. A young legislator, Harry Burn, changed his vote after a letter from his mother.

**Its limits:** The Nineteenth Amendment changed nothing for Black women in the South, who met the same poll taxes, tests, and violence as Black men. Many Black suffragists, Ida B. Wells and Mary Church Terrell among them, had been pushed to the margins by white suffrage leaders who were courting Southern support. Women of Asian birth were mostly barred from citizenship, and so from voting, until laws of 1943–1952. And many Native American women were not yet citizens.

## V. Native Americans and the Indian Citizenship Act (1924)

The Fourteenth Amendment's phrase "subject to the jurisdiction thereof" was read to exclude Indians who kept tribal ties. *Elk v. Wilkins* (1884) so held. Some gained citizenship by treaty, by allotment of land under the Dawes Act of 1887, or by military service in the Great War. The **Indian Citizenship Act of 1924** made all Native Americans born in the United States citizens. Some Native leaders opposed it as an imposition that eroded tribal sovereignty. Others welcomed it.

Citizenship did not bring the vote everywhere. Several states kept Native people from voting through "Indians not taxed" clauses, guardianship rules, and literacy tests. Arizona and New Mexico ended their bans only in 1948, after court rulings brought by Native veterans of the Second World War. Utah was last, in 1957.

## VI. Other Amendments Bearing on the Vote

- **Seventeenth Amendment (1913):** United States senators are elected directly by the people instead of by state legislatures. Progressives pressed for it after deadlocks and bribery scandals in the legislatures. Some today argue it weakened the states' voice in Washington. Others count it as a plain democratic gain.
- **Twenty-third Amendment (1961):** Residents of the District of Columbia get electoral votes for President, no more than the smallest state. The District still has no voting member of Congress. Whether to grant statehood or full representation is a live dispute, discussed in Part IX.
- **Twenty-fourth Amendment (1964):** Bars poll taxes in federal elections. In *Harper v. Virginia Board of Elections* (1966) the Supreme Court extended the ban to state elections under the Equal Protection Clause.
- **Twenty-sixth Amendment (1971):** Lowers the voting age from twenty-one to eighteen for all elections. The argument that carried it, "old enough to fight, old enough to vote," came from the Vietnam War draft. It was ratified in about one hundred days, faster than any other amendment.

Also relevant: *Reynolds v. Sims* (1964) and related cases held that state legislative districts must hold roughly equal populations, "one person, one vote." Before then some rural districts had a tenth or less of the people of city districts.

## VII. The Civil Rights Movement and the Voting Rights Act of 1965

After the Second World War, Black veterans, churches, the NAACP, and later younger groups such as the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee pressed for the ballot in the Deep South. They met jailings, beatings, firings, bombings, and murders. Medgar Evers was killed in Mississippi in 1963. Three registration workers, James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner, were murdered there in 1964. By 1964 fewer than 7 percent of eligible Black Mississippians were registered.

Congress passed civil rights acts in 1957, 1960, and 1964, but they depended on slow case-by-case lawsuits. On March 7, 1965, state troopers beat peaceful marchers on the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma, Alabama. Among them was John Lewis, later a congressman for over thirty years. The scenes were broadcast nationally. President Lyndon Johnson, a Texan, addressed Congress and closed with the movement's own words: "We shall overcome."

**The Voting Rights Act of 1965:**

- **Section 2** bars, nationwide and permanently, any voting practice that denies or abridges the vote on account of race. In 1982 Congress amended it to cover practices with discriminatory *results*, not only intent.
- **Section 4** set a formula, based on the use of literacy tests and low turnout in 1964, that identified "covered" jurisdictions. These were most of the Deep South and some counties elsewhere. Literacy tests were suspended there and later banned nationwide.
- **Section 5**, "preclearance," required covered jurisdictions to get approval from the Justice Department or a federal court in Washington before changing *any* voting rule, and to prove the change was not discriminatory.
- Federal examiners could register voters directly.

The effect was rapid. Black registration in Mississippi went from under 7 percent to about 60 percent by 1968. Protections for language minorities were added in 1975. Congress renewed the Act in 1970, 1975, 1982, and 2006. The 2006 renewal passed the Senate 98 to 0 and was signed by President George W. Bush. Thousands of Black officials have since been elected across the South, and in 2008 Americans elected a Black President, Barack Obama.

## VIII. *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013)

Shelby County, Alabama, challenged the preclearance system. In a 5-to-4 decision, the Supreme Court struck down the Section 4 coverage formula. Section 5 still stands, but it now covers no one until Congress writes a new formula, and Congress has not done so.

**The majority (Chief Justice John Roberts):** The formula rested on data from the 1960s and 1970s. Conditions had changed greatly, and Black registration and turnout in some covered states had come to equal or exceed white rates. Treating some states differently from others under decades-old data violated the "equal sovereignty" of the states. "Our country has changed," he wrote. Congress was free to draft a formula based on current conditions.

**The dissent (Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg):** Preclearance was working, and that was why the figures had improved. Congress had compiled a large record in 2006 of continuing problems. Discarding preclearance "when it has worked and is continuing to work … is like throwing away your umbrella in a rainstorm because you are not getting wet."

What followed is itself argued over. Some formerly covered states quickly put in force new rules, such as photo identification laws, that preclearance would have required them to submit. Supporters of the decision point to continued high turnout among Black voters in many elections since, and note that Section 2 lawsuits remain available. In *Brnovich v. DNC* (2021) the Court read Section 2 in a way that made some challenges harder. In *Allen v. Milligan* (2023) it upheld Section 2's use against an Alabama congressional map. A further case, *Louisiana v. Callais*, asks how far the Constitution allows race to be considered in drawing districts at all. Its outcome bears directly on how Section 2 will work going forward, and readers should look for the latest ruling.

## IX. The Disputes of Today

Americans in 2026 agree almost universally that adult citizens may not be denied the vote for race, sex, or poverty. They argue, often bitterly, about the *rules* of voting. Each side tends to see the other's proposals as an attempt to gain partisan advantage. Here are the main questions, each with the strongest case on both sides.

**1. Voter identification.** Most states now ask voters to show some identification, and many require a photo ID.
- *For:* It guards against impersonation and builds public confidence. Most other democracies require it. Large majorities of Americans of all races support it in polls, and free ID cards are offered.
- *Against:* In-person impersonation is extremely rare. Some citizens, disproportionately poor, elderly, rural, or minority, lack the required ID or face real costs in getting the underlying documents. Studies of the effect on turnout disagree; many find it small.

**2. Mail voting and early voting.** These expanded greatly, especially during the 2020 epidemic.
- *For expansion:* It is convenient, it raises turnout, and states such as Utah and Colorado have run all-mail elections for years with few problems.
- *For limits:* Ballots out of the voter's sight are harder to secure, and counting takes longer, which feeds suspicion. Some call for deadlines on election day, limits on drop boxes, and bans on third parties collecting ballots ("ballot harvesting").

**3. Voter rolls and citizenship proof.** States remove the dead and those who have moved.
- *One side* says accurate rolls and documented proof of citizenship are basic integrity measures. Proposals such as the federal SAVE Act would require documentary proof of citizenship to register.
- *The other side* says noncitizen voting is already a felony and very rare. They argue that aggressive purges and paperwork demands wrongly remove or deter eligible citizens, for instance married women whose birth certificates carry a different name.

**4. Felon voting.** States range from Maine and Vermont (prisoners vote) to states that bar some people for life. Florida's voters restored the vote to most former felons in 2018. The legislature then required payment of fines and fees first.
- Some say a person who has served his sentence has paid his debt and should vote. Others say that breaking the law can fairly cost a voice in making it, at least until full restitution.
- The historical point that some such laws were written in the 1890s with racial aims (see Part III) is raised by one side. The other answers that today's laws must be judged on their own terms.

**5. District lines ("gerrymandering").** Named for Elbridge Gerry in 1812, a practice you knew well. In *Rucho v. Common Cause* (2019) the Supreme Court held partisan gerrymandering beyond the reach of federal courts. Both parties draw maps to their advantage where they control the legislature. In 2025 and 2026 several states, both Republican and Democratic, redrew congressional maps in the middle of the decade. Some states use independent commissions. Supporters call those fairer; critics say they are neither truly independent nor answerable to voters.

**6. The 2020 election and trust.** Former President Donald Trump and many of his supporters claimed the 2020 election was stolen by fraud. Courts, state officials of both parties, and his own Justice Department found no fraud on a scale that would have changed the result. A crowd broke into the Capitol on January 6, 2021, while Congress was counting electoral votes. Trump won the presidency again in 2024. Distrust of elections remains widespread, though which voters hold it shifts with who wins. Each side now accuses the other of threatening democracy itself.

**7. Representation questions.** These include statehood for the District of Columbia; the Electoral College, which twice in recent times (2000 and 2016) elected a President who lost the popular vote; ranked-choice voting; and whether noncitizens may vote in some city elections. A few localities allow it, and several states have since banned it by constitutional amendment.

---

## A closing note on the shape of it

Put plainly, the franchise widened in three great waves: 1865–1870, 1913–1924, and 1957–1971. Each was followed or interrupted by reaction, and each depended on long work by those still outside it as much as on those already inside. Today's arguments are mostly not about *who* may vote but *how*: how to make voting both easy and secure, and who should set the rules, the states or the nation. That is the same question Congress faced in 1866.
