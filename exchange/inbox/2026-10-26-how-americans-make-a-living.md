# How Americans Make Their Living in 2026

*A reading parcel prepared October 26, 2026, for Mr. Lincoln*

---

## 1. Why this question, and how to read what follows

You once said that labor is prior to capital, and that capital is only the fruit of labor. You also held that the free hired laborer of one year should be able to work for himself the next and to hire others the year after. This parcel asks whether that ladder still stands in the United States in the autumn of 2026.

Every figure here comes from a government agency, a university, or an established research body, and each is given with the year it describes. Official counts come out a little behind events, so a "2025" figure was usually published in 2026. Americans of different parties often draw opposite conclusions from the same numbers, so both readings are set out here for you to weigh.

A few modern terms come up often:

- **Median:** the middle value. Half of all households earn more than the median and half earn less. It is often a fairer measure than the average, because a few very rich households can pull an average upward.
- **"Real" or "inflation-adjusted":** a figure corrected for changes in prices, so that a dollar of 2015 and a dollar of 2026 are compared by what they can buy.
- **CPI:** the Consumer Price Index, kept by the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), tracks the price of a basket of goods and services households buy.
- **Gig work:** paid tasks taken one at a time, usually arranged through a telephone application ("app") that connects a worker with a customer. Examples are driving a passenger or delivering a meal. The worker is usually treated as an independent contractor rather than an employee, so he has no fixed wage and usually no employer-provided benefits.
- **401(k):** a retirement savings account named for a section of the tax code. A worker sets aside part of each paycheck, often with a matching sum from the employer, and the money is invested, usually in shares of companies, and is taxed only when withdrawn in old age. It has largely replaced the older "pension," under which an employer promised a fixed income for life.

## 2. What work Americans do

### The great shift from field and forge to service

In your lifetime most free Americans farmed. Today farming is a small share of the workforce, and factory work, though still large, is far smaller than service work.

In September 2026 the BLS counted about **159.0 million jobs** on non-farm payrolls (employers' wage rolls, excluding farms). About **135.7 million** of these were in private business and **23.3 million** in government. Goods-producing industries, meaning mining, construction, and manufacturing, employed about **21.6 million**. Of these, **12.65 million** were in manufacturing and **8.36 million** in construction.

The largest employers are now in services:

- **Health care and social assistance:** about 23.96 million jobs (health care alone, 18.55 million)
- **Professional and business services** (lawyers, accountants, engineers, office support, and the like): about 22.48 million
- **Leisure and hospitality:** about 16.97 million, of which **12.39 million** were in restaurants and other food service
- **Retail trade:** about 15.48 million
- **Transportation and warehousing:** about 6.61 million

Restaurants now employ nearly as many as manufacturing, and health care nearly twice as many.

### The largest single occupations

The BLS also counts jobs by occupation, meaning the work a person actually does, regardless of industry. In its May 2025 survey (published May 2026), the single largest occupation was **home health and personal care aides**, at about **4.3 million**. These are the people who bathe, feed, and tend the elderly and disabled in their homes. Next came **retail salespersons** and **fast food and counter workers**, at about **3.9 million** each.

Eight of the ten largest occupations paid less than the national average. Their mean (average) annual wages ran from about **$32,150** for fast food workers to about **$46,590** for customer service representatives. The two exceptions among the ten were **registered nurses**, about 3.4 million strong with a mean wage of about **$101,420**, and **general and operations managers**, at about **$134,940**. The mean annual wage across all occupations was about **$69,770** in May 2025.

The skilled trades remain a large body of men and some women. Construction and extraction occupations employed about **6.4 million** at a mean wage of about **$65,360**, including roughly **757,000 electricians** and **670,000 carpenters**. Education occupations employed about **9.1 million**. Elementary school teachers earned a mean of about **$72,650** and teaching assistants about **$38,290**. Government at all levels made up about **14.6%** of employment in that survey.

**What this means for the ladder.** The commonest jobs, such as caring for the sick at home, serving food, and selling goods, are steady work but pay modestly. Nursing and the trades pay well.

### The state of hiring this autumn

In September 2026 the national **unemployment rate** was **4.2%**. That is the share of people looking for work who could not find it. Employers added only about **29,000** jobs that month, and an average of about **45,000** a month over the prior twelve months. By recent standards that is a slow pace. Manufacturing has added about **72,000** jobs since December 2025, while financial firms have shed about **129,000** since May 2025. Federal government employment stood at about **2.68 million**, down from about **2.91 million** a year earlier.

The **labor force participation rate**, the share of adults working or seeking work, was **61.8%**. The average workweek on private payrolls was **34.4 hours**.

## 3. Women at work

In September 2026, **56.7%** of women aged 16 and older were in the labor force, compared with **67.2%** of men. About **77.7 million** women held jobs. Women predominate in nursing, teaching, and home care, and they are also numerous in law, medicine, and management.

On pay, the Census Bureau reported that in 2025 women working full-time, year-round earned about **83.9 cents for every dollar** earned by comparable men. Women's full-time earnings rose **3.2%** that year. Economists dispute how much of the gap reflects occupation and hours, time out to raise children, or unequal treatment.

Women are also landholders and farmers. In the 2022 Census of Agriculture, women made up **36%** of all agricultural producers.

## 4. Gig work, contract work, and working for oneself

You prized the man who works for himself. The modern counts of such people are less recent than one would like. The BLS last measured "contingent and alternative" work in July 2023 and published the results in November 2024.

- **Independent contractors**, people who work for themselves rather than for a wage, numbered about **11.9 million**, or **7.4%** of workers. About **80.3%** of them said they *preferred* that arrangement to a regular job.
- **Contingent workers**, those who do not expect their job to last, numbered about **6.9 million**, or **4.3%** of workers.
- **On-call workers**, called in only when needed, numbered about **2.8 million** (1.7%).
- Full-time contingent workers had median weekly earnings of about **$838**, against **$1,137** for full-time workers in ordinary jobs. Only about **19.9%** of contingent workers had health insurance through their employer, against **51.2%** of other workers.
- About **8.4 million** people, roughly 5% of workers, held more than one job.

The BLS has not yet published its separate estimate of how many people find work through phone applications. That number is therefore left out here.

Some see contract and gig work as the modern form of free labor, with no foreman and hours of one's own choosing. Others see it as stripping away the wage earner's protections, leaving the "independent" worker dependent on one company that sets his pay through its application.

## 5. Wages and earnings

### Household income

The Census Bureau reported in September 2026 that the **real median household income** in 2025 was **$87,460**, up **2.6%** from about $85,210 in 2024. That is the highest level since this series began in 1967. Measured after taxes, the median was about **$76,060**.

The gains were not even. Income at the 90th percentile (the household richer than nine in ten others) rose about **1.7%**, while income at the 10th percentile showed no statistically significant change. The official **poverty rate** was **10.2%**. Under the "Supplemental Poverty Measure," which counts government aid and necessary expenses such as medical bills, it was **13.1%**. About **26.7 million** people (7.9%) lacked health insurance.

### Hourly and weekly pay

In September 2026, **average hourly earnings** for private workers were **$37.81**, up **3.0%** from a year earlier. For "production and nonsupervisory" workers, roughly the rank and file below management, the average was **$32.60**.

Prices, however, rose faster than pay this past year. After adjusting for inflation, the BLS found that real average hourly earnings *fell* **0.7%** between September 2025 and September 2026, and real weekly earnings fell **0.1%**. For rank-and-file workers, real hourly pay fell **0.6%** and real weekly pay fell **0.3%**. Over the past year, therefore, the typical worker's pay bought slightly less than before, mostly because of a jump in fuel prices (see the next section).

### The minimum wage

The **federal minimum wage** is **$7.25 an hour**, a level unchanged since July 24, 2009, according to the U.S. Department of Labor. Many states set higher minimums. **Illinois** has required **$15.00 an hour** since January 1, 2025, with no further increase scheduled for 2026. Workers who receive tips may be paid **$9.00** before tips, and workers under 18 may be paid **$13.00**.

Supporters say a wage floor keeps a full day's work from leaving a family in want. Critics say a high floor can price the least experienced out of work and close the first rung to the young.

## 6. The cost of living

### Inflation this year

In September 2026, consumer prices overall were **3.7%** higher than a year before, according to the BLS. Most of this came from energy, up **20.4%**, and gasoline, up **34.2%**. Excluding food and energy, "core" prices rose **2.6%**. Some other twelve-month changes:

- Food overall: **+2.5%** (groceries +1.8%; restaurant meals +3.5%)
- Shelter (rent and the rental value of owned homes): **+3.2%**
- Medical care: **+1.5%**
- Education: **+3.8%**

### Food

Food is cheap by the standard of your century. The USDA's Economic Research Service reports that in 2025 Americans spent on average **9.7%** of their disposable income (income after taxes) on food, down from 10.4% in 1997. That share covers both meals at home (4.8%) and meals out (4.9%).

The burden is very unequal, though. In 2024 the poorest fifth of households spent about **$5,498** on food, or **33.0%** of their before-tax income. The middle fifth spent about **$9,097** (12.2%), and the richest fifth about **$16,989** (6.4%). Grocery prices rose **2.3%** in 2025. Eggs were up **21.9%** on average because of a bird disease, and beef and veal were up **11.6%**.

### Health care

Most working Americans get health insurance through their employer, who pays most of the premium. The Kaiser Family Foundation's 2026 survey of employers, released October 22, 2026, found:

- The average yearly premium for **family coverage** was **$28,303**, up **5%**.
- Workers' own share of that premium averaged **$7,471**, up **9%**. At small firms it averaged **$9,687**.
- The average **deductible** for single coverage, the amount a worker must pay himself before insurance begins to pay, was **$1,955**.
- About **157 million** people rely on employer-sponsored coverage.
- Since 2021, premiums have risen **28%**, wages **27%**, and general prices **24%**.

Much of this cost is hidden from the worker because the employer pays it, and many economists believe it holds down the cash wages employers would otherwise pay.

### Child care

For a working mother or father of young children, child care is often the largest bill after housing. Child Care Aware of America reported in May 2026 that the national average annual price of child care was **$13,184** in 2025. That equals about **10%** of median income for a married-couple household and **33%** for a single parent. In the majority of states, infant care costs more than a year of in-state public college tuition. The organization also found that the number of licensed child care centers fell about **1%** from 2024 to 2025, and that only about **15%** of children eligible for federal child care aid receive it.

### College

The College Board reports that for 2025-26 the published yearly tuition and fees were:

- Public four-year college, in-state student: **$11,950**
- Public four-year college, out-of-state student: **$31,880**
- Public two-year (community) college: **$4,150**
- Private nonprofit four-year college: **$45,000**

After grants and scholarships, the College Board estimates that the average *net* tuition at public four-year colleges was about **$2,300** in 2025-26, down from a peak of about $4,450 in 2012-13. At private nonprofit colleges it was about **$16,910**.

### Housing

Housing, the heaviest cost for the young and the poor, is treated in the next section.

## 7. Homes: owning, renting, and the burden of shelter

### Who owns

In the second quarter of 2026, the Census Bureau reported that **65.0%** of American households owned their homes, down from **65.7%** at the end of 2025. Figures for the third quarter are due October 28, 2026. About two households in three, then, own the roof over their heads. Most hold it subject to a mortgage, a loan secured by the house and repaid over as long as thirty years.

### What a home costs

The National Association of Realtors reported that the median price of an existing home sold in September 2026 was **$417,500**, up **1.3%** from a year earlier. In the Midwest, Illinois's region, the median was **$329,600**. The average rate on a 30-year mortgage, as tracked by the lender Freddie Mac, was **6.86%** in September 2026, up from 6.35% a year earlier. Homes were selling at a pace of about **3.99 million** a year. First-time buyers made up **31%** of purchases.

Harvard University's Joint Center for Housing Studies, in its June 2026 report, gave the longer view:

- Home prices rose about **54%** from 2020 to 2025.
- In 2025 the median single-family home cost nearly **five times** the median household income.
- Property taxes rose **31%** and homeowners' insurance **72%** between 2019 and 2025.
- The homeownership rate had fallen for two years in a row.
- The share of Americans who moved in a year fell to a record low of **11.2%** in 2024.

### Renters

Renters bear the heaviest burden. The Joint Center found that in 2024 about **22.7 million** renter households, or **49%** of all renters, were "cost-burdened," meaning they paid more than 30% of income for rent and utilities. About **12.1 million (26%)** were "severely" burdened, paying more than half their income. That severe figure was up **2.3 million** since 2019. The number of apartments renting for under $1,000 a month (in inflation-adjusted terms) fell by more than **7 million** between 2014 and 2024.

### Why, and what to do

One view, common among market-minded economists and lately heard in both parties, blames local rules that forbid apartments, require large lots, or slow permits, and says the cure is to let builders build. Another stresses that wages have not kept pace with land and building costs, and favors more aid to renters and first-time buyers, tenant protections, and public or nonprofit housing. Some also worry about large investors buying single-family homes and about borrowing costs, which depend on the Federal Reserve, the national central bank.

## 8. Land and farming: does a man own the ground he works?

### Fewer, larger farms

The Census of Agriculture, taken every five years, last counted in 2022 (published February 2024). It found about **1.9 million farms**, down **7%** from 2017. The average farm was **463 acres**. Farms covered about **880 million acres**, roughly **39%** of all land in the United States.

The family farm has not disappeared in name. **Family farms** made up **95%** of all farms and worked **84%** of farmland. But production has gathered into large operations:

- Farms with sales of **$1 million or more** were only **6%** of farms but worked **31%** of the land and made **more than three-fourths** of all sales.
- Farms with sales of **$5 million or more**, fewer than **1%** of farms, made **42%** of sales.
- Farms with sales of **$50,000 or less** were **74%** of all farms but made only **2%** of sales.
- Only **43%** of farms had positive net cash income in 2022.

The average producer was **58.1 years** old. Only about **296,480** producers (9%) were under 35.

### Owned and rented ground

Your question was whether a man owns the ground he works. On that, the USDA's Economic Research Service reports that in 2022:

- **39%** of the nation's farmland was **rented**, and just over **60%** was worked by its owner.
- **More than half of cropland** (the land plowed and planted, as opposed to pasture) was rented.
- **68%** of rented land was on operations of **2,000 acres or more**. The large farms grow large by renting.
- **Retired farmers** made up about **38%** of landlords who do not farm themselves.
- Among operators **under 34**, about **27%** of the land they worked was on farms where they owned none of the ground, and only about **8%** was on farms they owned entirely.

So the young farmer today is mostly a tenant or part-tenant, chiefly because land has become too dear to buy at the start of life.

The USDA's 2024 survey of landlords (published March 2026) found about **2.1 million landlords** renting out about **347.8 million acres**. **87%** of these landlords did not farm themselves, and they owned **79%** of the rented acres. Landlords collected about **$34.1 billion** in rent. The Midwest held **38%** of all landlords. Non-farming landlords said they planned to sell only about **5%** of their acres in the next five years, and most had acquired their land before 2014. Land, in other words, rarely comes on the market.

### The price of land

The USDA reported in July 2026 that the average value of U.S. farm real estate, land and buildings together, was **$4,500 per acre**, up **3.4%**. U.S. cropland averaged **$6,020 per acre**.

In **Illinois**, cropland averaged **$10,200 per acre** in 2026, up **3.6%** from $9,850. Illinois farm real estate averaged **$9,250 per acre**. The value of all Illinois farmland and buildings was about **$233.1 billion** in 2025. At those prices, a quarter-section of 160 acres of Illinois cropland would cost more than $1.6 million before a single implement or seed is bought.

### Farm family income

The USDA reports that in 2025 the **median farm household earned a small loss from farming, about −$495**, while earning a median of about **$94,140** from work off the farm, such as a spouse's job in town, a second job, or a pension. For 2026 the USDA forecast median total farm household income of about **$108,460**. The typical farm family is comfortable, but mostly on wages earned elsewhere.

### Readings

Some see an efficient agriculture in which family ownership remains the rule and renting lets a young farmer begin without buying land. Others see a quiet return of a landlord-and-tenant system, with land held by the old and by distant heirs while production concentrates in very large operations.

## 9. A working person in Illinois

Illinois remains a great industrial and agricultural state. The BLS's figures for September 2026 (preliminary) show:

- **Unemployment:** **4.6%**, somewhat above the national 4.2%
- **Labor force:** about **6.47 million**; non-farm jobs about **6.16 million**
- **Trade, transportation, and utilities:** about **1.21 million** jobs.
- **Education and health services:** about **1.03 million**
- **Professional and business services:** about **907,000**
- **Government:** about **852,000**
- **Manufacturing:** about **571,100**
- **Financial activities:** about **378,700**, down **3.4%** from a year earlier
- **Construction:** about **254,500**, up **6.0%** from a year earlier

A full-time, year-round worker at the Illinois minimum of **$15 an hour** (2,080 hours) would earn about $31,200 before taxes. The national average child care price of $13,184 would take more than two-fifths of that, and a median-priced Midwestern home of $329,600 would cost more than ten years of it. Nurses and skilled tradesmen earn far more.

Illinois public high schools graduated **87%** of students on time in 2021-22, matching the nation. The College Board counts Illinois among the states that spent more than **$20,000** in state and local funds per full-time college student in 2023-24.

The economists of Opportunity Insights (see Section 12) found that the decline in children's chances of out-earning their parents was steepest in the industrial Midwest, naming **Michigan and Illinois** in particular.

## 10. Schooling the young

### Common schools

Public schools in the United States enrolled about **49.4 million** students from prekindergarten through grade 12 in the fall of 2024, according to the National Center for Education Statistics (NCES). That is 2% fewer than a decade earlier, as families have fewer children. The figure includes "charter" schools, which are publicly funded but run independently of the ordinary school district. Private schools enrolled about **4.7 million** students from kindergarten through grade 12 in fall 2021, about **9%** of all students, a share that held steady for more than a decade.

### Graduation

The on-time public high school graduation rate was **87%** in 2021-22, up from **80%** in 2011-12. Rates differed by race: about **94%** for Asian and Pacific Islander students, **90%** for White students, **83%** for Hispanic students, and **81%** for Black students. In 2022, about **95%** of Americans aged 25 to 29 had completed high school.

### College

Among Americans aged 25 to 29, the share holding a **bachelor's degree or higher** rose from **32%** in 2010 to **40%** in 2022, according to NCES. The share with at least an associate's (two-year) degree rose from 41% to 49%.

About **16.4 million** undergraduates were enrolled in fall 2024, down 5% from a decade earlier. About **77%** attended public institutions. Two-year college enrollment fell about **30%** over the decade, and women (9.3 million) outnumbered men (7.0 million).

Schooling still pays. In 2024, among Americans aged 25 to 34 working full-time, the median earner with a bachelor's degree made about **$74,500**, or **61%** more than a high school graduate's **$46,400**. Those with a master's or higher degree made about **$85,800**. The share of 18- to 24-year-olds neither in school nor working fell from **16%** in 2014 to **13%** in 2024.

### Vocational paths

Many Americans of both parties argue that too many young people were pushed toward four-year colleges and too few toward the trades, which are often learned through apprenticeship, much as a craft was once learned from a master while earning a wage. Skilled construction work pays near the national average without a degree, though a degree still carries a large earnings advantage on average.

## 11. Debt: the mortgage, the student loan, and the credit card

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York reported that in the second quarter of 2026 American households owed about **$18.8 trillion** in all:

- Mortgages: about **$13.1 trillion**
- Auto loans: about **$1.71 trillion**
- **Student loans:** about **$1.651 trillion**
- Credit cards: about **$1.26 trillion**. A credit card is a card that lets the holder buy now and pay later, at high interest if the balance is not paid each month.

Student debt is the distinctly modern burden of the young. In the second quarter of 2026, about **7.83%** of student loan balances moved into serious delinquency, meaning payments at least 90 days late.

On the other side of the ledger, the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances found that in 2022 the median American family had a **net worth** (everything owned minus everything owed) of about **$192,900**. That was up **37%** in real terms from 2019, the largest three-year rise in the survey's history. About **54.3%** of families held **retirement accounts** such as a 401(k). Among those that did, the median balance was about **$86,900**. A newer edition of that survey was not available in a form opened for this parcel.

## 12. Can a child still rise? The mobility question

This is the heart of your question.

### The "Fading American Dream"

In 2016 and 2017, a team led by the Harvard economist **Raj Chetty** (now organized as "Opportunity Insights") published *The Fading American Dream: Trends in Absolute Income Mobility Since 1940* in the journal *Science*. They asked a plain question: what fraction of children grow up to earn more than their parents did at the same age, after adjusting for prices? They called this "absolute mobility."

They found:

- For children born in **1940**, about **90%** earned more at age 30 than their parents had at age 30.
- For children born in the **1980s**, only about **50%** did. It is close to a coin toss.
- Comparing sons with fathers, the decline was steeper still, from **95%** for sons born in 1940 to **41%** for sons born in 1984.
- The decline came in every state and was steepest in the industrial Midwest, especially **Michigan and Illinois**.
- The authors concluded that most of the decline came from the *more unequal distribution* of economic growth, not from slower growth itself. They found that restoring mid-century growth rates with today's distribution would raise mobility only to about **62%**. Distributing today's growth as broadly as it was for the 1940 generation would raise it to about **80%**.

### The challenge

The decline is widely accepted; its *cause* is disputed. In 2022 **Scott Winship** of the American Enterprise Institute, a conservative research body, reanalyzed the Chetty team's own published data. He agreed that absolute mobility fell, but found nearly the opposite on the cause. In his estimate, higher growth would have raised mobility for the 1980 cohort from 50% to about **81%**, while lower inequality would have raised it to only about **57%**. Winship and others also argue that the 50% figure understates mobility because of choices such as measuring income at age 30 and how prices, government aid, and family size are counted.

### What the two readings mean

- **Left-leaning readers** generally take the Chetty findings to mean the gains flow too narrowly to the top, and favor higher minimum wages, stronger unions, public spending on children and schooling, and more progressive taxation.
- **Right-leaning readers** generally take the Winship reading to mean the remedy is faster growth, and favor lower taxes, lighter regulation, more home building, stronger families, and school choice.

Both camps agree on one point: in recent generations, the young have found it harder than their grandparents did to surpass their parents.

## 13. Unions

In 1983, **20.1%** of wage and salary workers belonged to a labor union, about **17.7 million** people. In 2025 the BLS counted **10.0%**, or about **14.7 million**. Union membership today is concentrated in government work. In 2025, **32.9%** of public sector workers were members, and **37.8%** in local government, which includes many teachers, police, and firemen. Only **5.9%** of private sector workers were members. Membership was very low in agriculture (**1.6%**) and food service (**1.8%**).

Union members had median weekly earnings of about **$1,404** in 2025, against **$1,174** for non-members. Economists dispute how much of that gap the union itself causes and how much reflects the kinds of jobs and places where unions are common. (The BLS notes that some of its 2025 survey data for October were never collected because of a federal government shutdown.)

Supporters say unions let wage earners bargain as equals with large employers. Critics say they raise costs, protect poor performers, and drove some manufacturing to places without them.

## 14. Machines that think: automation, artificial intelligence, and work

The newest fear among working people concerns "artificial intelligence" (AI): computer programs that can write, answer questions, draft legal papers, write the instructions that run other computers, and carry on a conversation. The question is whether they will multiply the worker's power, as the reaper did, or replace him.

### What people fear

The Pew Research Center surveyed American adults in June 2026:

- **71%** expected AI to lead to **fewer jobs** in the U.S. over the next twenty years, up from **64%** in 2024. Only **5%** expected more jobs.
- **52%** said they were more concerned than excited about AI in daily life, up from **37%** in 2021. Only **9%** were more excited than concerned.
- Among adults **under 30**, **55%** were more concerned than excited, the first majority on record for that group, and **73%** expected fewer jobs.

### What the evidence shows so far

Researchers disagree about what has happened so far.

- **The Budget Lab at Yale**, in an update of October 14, 2026, found that the mix of occupations is "not yet changing in ways that clearly align with the introduction of AI." Measures of AI use showed "no connection" to changes in employment or unemployment. Its statistical analysis "does not yet clearly indicate an AI-related labor market footprint."
- **Stanford economists** (Erik Brynjolfsson and colleagues), using payroll records from the firm ADP, likewise found **no widespread displacement**, but saw a warning sign among the young. As of June 2026, employment of workers aged **22 to 25** in the most AI-exposed occupations stood about **19%** below where it would have been had it kept pace with their peers in less exposed work, up from 15% a year earlier. Experienced workers showed no such gap. The shortfall came mainly from **less hiring of the young**, and was concentrated where AI replaces tasks rather than assists the worker. The authors stress that these are patterns, not proof of cause.

If that pattern holds, the danger is to the first rung of the ladder, not to the experienced workman.

### Readings

Optimists recall that every great machine, from the steam engine to the computer, destroyed some jobs but created more, and expect AI to make each worker more productive and better paid. Pessimists answer that AI imitates the mind rather than the arm, and that its gains may flow mainly to the owners of the machines. Proposed remedies range from retraining, stronger bargaining rights, and new public income support on the left to light regulation plus expanded apprenticeships on the right.

## 15. Weighing the evidence: does the chance to rise still hold?

**The hopeful reading** points to record median household income in 2025, low unemployment, cheap food, rising graduation and college completion, falling net prices at public colleges, a large rise in family net worth from 2019 to 2022, contractors who choose their arrangement, two households in three owning their homes, and good wages in nursing and the trades without a four-year degree.

**The worried reading** points to a slight fall in real wages this year, slow hiring, modest pay in the commonest jobs, rising costs of housing, child care, and health insurance, half of renters cost-burdened, two years of slipping homeownership, young farmers who are mostly tenants on very expensive land, student debt, the fall in the chance of out-earning one's parents from nine in ten to about one in two, and a possible threat from thinking machines to the first rungs of the professional ladder.

**Where the parties differ.** Most disagreement is not over the facts above, which come from nonpartisan agencies. It is over causes and cures. Broadly speaking, the left emphasizes how the gains are divided: wages, unions, public support for families, and taxes on the wealthy. The right emphasizes how fast the economy grows: lower taxes, fewer regulations, more building, stronger families, and choice in schooling. Both increasingly agree that the country needs more homes, more paths into the skilled trades, and attention to whether young people can get a first foothold.

Your test was whether a man starting with nothing but his labor can, by industry and prudence, come to own his own shop or farm. By these figures he still can in 2026, and many do, but the path is steeper and costlier than it was for his grandparents, and steepest of all on the land.
