# What Became of the Indian Nations of the West

*A reading parcel prepared October 28, 2026, for Mr. Lincoln*

---

## 1. Why this question, and how to read what follows

You asked what became of the Indian nations of the West after April 1865: the treaties, the wars, the reservations, the schools, and their condition today. You asked to hear both their own accounts and the government's. Your reason was that you signed the Homestead law and the Pacific Railway law in 1862, praised the free man's chance to rise on cheap land, and never asked whose land it was.

This parcel runs roughly in order of time, from 1862 to 2026. Where a figure comes from a government report, a court opinion, or a census table, the source and its year are given. Some figures from the 1800s are estimates, and historians quarrel over them. Where that is so, the range is given rather than one number.

The parcel quotes Native writers and leaders in their own words. It also sets out the views of officials and reformers who believed, often sincerely, that they were saving the Indian from extinction. Historians today are mostly harsh about the results of federal Indian policy. They disagree a good deal more about the motives behind it, and about which words fit what happened. Section 15 sets out those disagreements.

A few terms come up often:

- **Tribe or nation:** a self-governing Native people. The federal government now formally recognizes **574** of them (Bureau of Indian Affairs, 2024). Many tribes call themselves nations.
- **Reservation:** land reserved to a tribe, usually by treaty, statute, or executive order. The Census Bureau counted **325** federally recognized reservations and off-reservation trust lands in 2024.
- **Trust land:** land whose legal title is held by the United States on behalf of a tribe or an individual Indian. It generally cannot be sold or taxed by a state without federal consent.
- **Sovereignty:** in this setting, a tribe's inherent right to govern itself. The Supreme Court has called tribes "domestic dependent nations," a phrase from Chief Justice Marshall in 1831 that you may recall.
- **Allotment:** the policy of dividing tribal land held in common into individual parcels.
- **BIA:** the Bureau of Indian Affairs, the successor to the Indian Office you knew, still within the Department of the Interior.

## 2. The laws of 1862 and the land they opened

### The Homestead Act

The Homestead Act of May 20, 1862, offered 160 acres of the public domain to a settler who lived on it and worked it for five years. Over the next seven decades roughly 1.6 million homestead claims were granted, covering about 270 million acres, or about a tenth of the land area of the United States (National Park Service, Homestead National Historical Park). Much of that land lay on the Great Plains.

The law could only give away land the United States counted as public domain. In the Plains, land got onto that list one of three ways: a treaty of cession, an act of Congress setting aside or shrinking a reservation, or war followed by one of those two. The homestead law did not take Indian land itself. It created the demand, and in a few decades it settled millions of farmers on land that had been Indian country within living memory. Historians of the West, such as Richard White, describe the homestead, the railroad, and the reservation as parts of one system. The settler needed the land cleared of its owners' claims, and the government cleared it.

### The Pacific Railway Acts

The Pacific Railway Act of July 1, 1862, and its 1864 amendment granted the Union Pacific and Central Pacific a right-of-way plus alternating sections of land along the line, ten sections per mile at first and twenty after 1864. Later grants to the Northern Pacific, the Kansas Pacific, the Atlantic and Pacific, and others followed the same pattern. Federal railroad land grants as a whole came to roughly 130 million acres. This figure is commonly cited by historians but was not confirmed from a primary page for this parcel.

The 1862 act directed the government to "extinguish as rapidly as may be the Indian titles to all lands falling under the operation of this act." The railroads brought soldiers and settlers quickly. They also brought hide hunters, who could ship bison hides east by rail (see Section 6). Cheyenne, Arapaho, and Lakota war parties attacked survey crews and construction camps in 1867 and after, because they understood what the line meant. The Northern Pacific's surveys along the Yellowstone in 1872 and 1873, guarded by the army, helped bring on the Lakota war of 1876.

### The Morrill Act

You signed a third law that year, the Morrill Act of July 2, 1862, which gave the states public land to fund colleges of agriculture and the mechanic arts. A 2020 investigation by the magazine *High Country News* traced the grant parcels and found that they came to about 10.7 million acres taken from nearly 250 tribes, bands, and communities. Some of that land was obtained by treaties in which the tribe was paid little or nothing. The university land-grant system the act created still exists, and several of those universities have since published statements about where their endowment land came from.

## 3. The treaty era, 1865 to 1871

### Sand Creek, the background to everything after

Your own term ended in the shadow of Sand Creek. On November 29, 1864, Colonel John Chivington led about 700 Colorado volunteers against a camp of Southern Cheyenne and Arapaho on Sand Creek, in the Colorado Territory. The camp's leaders, Black Kettle and White Antelope among them, believed they were under the army's protection. Black Kettle flew an American flag over his lodge. The National Park Service, which now keeps the site as a national historic site, estimates that about 230 people were killed, most of them women, children, and old men. Bodies were mutilated, and soldiers carried trophies back to Denver.

Two army officers, Captain Silas Soule and Lieutenant Joseph Cramer, refused to order their companies to fire. Soule's letters describing the killings reached Washington. In 1865 the Joint Committee on the Conduct of the War and a military commission both condemned the attack. The joint committee said Chivington had "deliberately planned and executed a foul and dastardly massacre." He was never tried, and he left the service. Soule testified against him and was murdered in Denver in April 1865.

Sand Creek turned many Cheyenne and Lakota who had favored peace toward war, and it shamed enough of Congress to start a peace policy. In 1865, Senator James Doolittle's committee began an inquiry into the condition of the tribes. Its 1867 report blamed most of the wars on "the aggressions of lawless white men."

### The Peace Commission and the treaties of 1867 and 1868

In 1867 Congress created an Indian Peace Commission. Its members included General William T. Sherman, Commissioner of Indian Affairs Nathaniel Taylor, and Senator John Henderson. The commission's purpose was to end the Plains wars by persuading the tribes to settle on defined reservations away from the railroad routes. In return the government would supply food, clothing, schools, and farm tools.

**Medicine Lodge (October 1867).** At Medicine Lodge Creek in Kansas, the commission signed treaties with the Kiowa, Comanche, Plains Apache, Southern Cheyenne, and Arapaho, assigning them reservations in the western Indian Territory, now Oklahoma. Article 12 of the Kiowa-Comanche treaty said no further cession of the reservation would be valid unless signed by three-fourths of the adult men of the tribes. That promise became the subject of a famous lawsuit (see Section 7). The Kiowa chief Satanta told the commissioners: "I love the land and the buffalo and will not part with it... I don't want to settle. I love to roam over the prairies."

**Fort Laramie (April to November 1868).** The Lakota under Red Cloud had fought the army to a standstill along the Bozeman Trail in 1866 and 1867. In the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868, the United States agreed to abandon its forts on the trail. Red Cloud signed only after the troops had left and the Lakota had burned the forts. The treaty created the **Great Sioux Reservation**, which covered all of present South Dakota west of the Missouri River, including the **Black Hills**. It was set apart for the "absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the Sioux. The treaty also recognized a larger "unceded Indian territory" in the Powder River country as closed to whites. It too required the signatures of three-fourths of the adult men for any future cession.

The **Navajo Treaty of 1868** ended the Navajo's confinement at the Bosque Redondo, which began during your administration (see Section 14), and let them return to part of their homeland. The Navajo still mark it as the treaty that brought them home.

### The Peace Policy

President Grant took office in 1869 and announced what was called his "Peace Policy." It placed the reservation agencies under the nomination of Protestant and Catholic church bodies, on the theory that clergymen would be more honest than political appointees. It also set up a Board of Indian Commissioners of unpaid philanthropists to watch over the purchase of supplies. Grant appointed Ely S. Parker, a Seneca who had served as his military secretary and wrote out the surrender terms at Appomattox, as the first Native Commissioner of Indian Affairs. Parker's enemies drove him from office in 1871 with charges of fraud. A House committee cleared him of them.

### The end of treaty-making, 1871

On March 3, 1871, a clause in the Indian Appropriations Act provided that no Indian nation "shall be acknowledged or recognized as an independent nation, tribe, or power with whom the United States may contract by treaty." The House of Representatives had long resented the fact that the Senate alone ratified treaties, while the House had to vote the money to fulfill them. The act did not cancel the treaties already made. The National Archives holds about 370 ratified Indian treaties. From then on, Congress dealt with tribes through statutes and "agreements," which it could change on its own.

## 4. The Plains wars

### The Black Hills and the Little Bighorn

In 1874 Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer led an army expedition into the Black Hills and reported gold "from the grass roots down." Thousands of miners poured into the Hills in breach of the 1868 treaty. The army made a feeble effort to keep them out and then gave up. In 1875 a government commission offered to buy the Hills. The Lakota refused. Late that year the Indian Office ordered all Lakota bands to report to their agencies by January 31, 1876, in the dead of winter, or be treated as hostile.

On June 25 and 26, 1876, Custer attacked a large encampment of Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Arapaho on the Little Bighorn River in Montana Territory. The war leaders Crazy Horse and Gall were in the camp, along with Sitting Bull, the Hunkpapa holy man. Custer and every man in the five companies under his direct command were killed, and in all about 268 soldiers, scouts, and civilians died. Native accounts gathered afterward, many of them recorded decades later, describe the fight as a defensive one: they believed their families were being attacked. The battle stunned the country during its centennial summer.

Within a year, the army had forced most of the bands onto the agencies. Sitting Bull went into Canada and returned in 1881. Crazy Horse surrendered in May 1877. He was bayoneted that September at Camp Robinson, Nebraska, in a struggle as soldiers tried to put him in a guardhouse.

### The taking of the Black Hills, 1877

In August 1876, before the war ended, Congress passed an appropriation saying the Sioux would receive no more rations until they gave up the Black Hills and the unceded territory. A commission led by George Manypenny, himself a former Commissioner of Indian Affairs, took the terms to the agencies. The Lakota called it "sign or starve." Only about 10 percent of the adult men signed, far short of the three-fourths the 1868 treaty required. Congress enacted the cession anyway on February 28, 1877. A century later this became the basis of the Sioux claim discussed in Section 11.

### Other wars of the 1870s

- **The Modoc War (1872–73)** in northern California and southern Oregon.
- **The Red River War (1874–75)** on the southern Plains. It ended the free life of the Comanche, Kiowa, and Southern Cheyenne. The Comanche leader Quanah Parker surrendered in 1875 and became a prosperous rancher and a go-between with the government.
- **The Nez Perce War (1877).** Bands under Chief Joseph and others retreated some 1,170 miles toward Canada before surrendering in Montana, forty miles from the border. The speech attributed to Joseph at the surrender ends: "From where the sun now stands I will fight no more forever." It was written down by an army officer, and scholars debate how closely it matches his words.
- **The Northern Cheyenne breakout (1878–79).** Northern Cheyenne sent to the Indian Territory fled north under Dull Knife and Little Wolf. Many were killed escaping from Fort Robinson in January 1879.
- **The Apache wars**, which ended with Geronimo's surrender in 1886. Geronimo's band, and the Apache scouts who had served the army, were sent as prisoners of war to Florida and Alabama and later to Fort Sill. They were held there until 1913.

### Standing Bear in court, 1879

In 1879 the Ponca chief Standing Bear left the Indian Territory without permission to bury his son in the tribe's old homeland in Nebraska, and he was arrested. Lawyers in Omaha sued for a writ of habeas corpus. Standing Bear told the court: "That hand is not the color of yours, but if I prick it, the blood will flow, and I shall feel pain... I am a man. The same God made us both." Judge Elmer Dundy ruled in *United States ex rel. Standing Bear v. Crook* that "an Indian is a 'person' within the meaning of the laws of the United States," and Standing Bear was freed. The case stirred eastern reform opinion, as did Helen Hunt Jackson's book *A Century of Dishonor* (1881).

### Wounded Knee, 1890

By the late 1880s the Lakota were confined to a reduced reservation. In 1889 another act broke the Great Sioux Reservation into six smaller ones. Rations were cut, and crops failed. Many Lakota took up the **Ghost Dance**, a religious movement begun by the Paiute prophet Wovoka. It promised that if the people danced and lived peaceably, the dead would return, the bison would come back, and the whites would be gone. Agents and newspapers called it an uprising. The army sent troops.

On December 15, 1890, Indian police sent to arrest Sitting Bull at Standing Rock killed him in a gunfight. On December 29, the Seventh Cavalry surrounded Big Foot's band of Miniconjou Lakota at Wounded Knee Creek on the Pine Ridge reservation and began to disarm them. A shot was fired, and the soldiers opened fire, with Hotchkiss guns among their weapons. Estimates of the Lakota dead run from about 150 to more than 250, many of them women and children, some killed miles from the camp. About 25 soldiers died, some likely from their own side's fire. Twenty soldiers received the Medal of Honor. Descendants have long asked Congress to rescind those medals. In 2024 the Defense Department reviewed the awards and announced it would leave them in place, a decision tribal leaders protested.

The Oglala holy man **Black Elk**, a young man at the time, told the poet John Neihardt in 1931:

> "I did not know then how much was ended. When I look back now from this high hill of my old age, I can still see the butchered women and children lying heaped and scattered all along the crooked gulch as plain as when I saw them with eyes still young. And I can see that something else died there in the bloody mud, and was buried in the blizzard. A people's dream died there."

(*Black Elk Speaks*, 1932. Scholars note that Neihardt shaped the wording, and that Black Elk was by then a practicing Catholic catechist. The book is a joint work.)

The Census Office's report on the 1890 census declared the frontier closed. Wounded Knee is commonly taken as the end of the Indian wars.

## 5. Numbers: how many people

Estimates of the Native population of what became the United States before European contact range from under one million to more than ten million, and the question is fiercely argued. Almost all scholars agree that by 1900 the federal census counted only about **237,000** American Indians in the continental United States, the low point. Most of that fall came before your time, from epidemic disease brought by Europeans, but war, hunger, removal, and reservation conditions all added to it.

The population then recovered. In the 2020 census, **3.7 million** people gave American Indian or Alaska Native as their only race, and **9.7 million** gave it alone or in combination with another race (Census Bureau, 2020 Census). The 2024 American Community Survey estimated **9.1 million** alone or in combination. The large jump in the combined count since 2010 partly reflects changes in how the census asks the question and how people choose to identify, not only births. Tribes set their own membership rules, and most require descent from people on a historical roll, so the census count and tribal enrollment differ.

## 6. The destruction of the bison

At the start of the 1800s the North American bison may have numbered in the tens of millions. Estimates of 30 million or more are common, though figures of 60 million are now thought too high. By 1889 the naturalist William Hornaday of the Smithsonian could find records of only about **1,091** wild bison in the United States.

The great slaughter came after 1870, when eastern tanneries found a way to turn bison hides into industrial leather. With the railroads in place, professional hide hunters killed millions a year on the southern Plains until about 1878, and then on the northern Plains until 1883. Earlier trade in robes, drought, cattle diseases, competition from horses, and Native hunting for the market had already thinned the herds.

Historians differ on whether the government set out to destroy the bison in order to defeat the tribes.

- Some, such as David Smits, argue that army commanders like Sherman and Sheridan understood the slaughter as a war measure, welcomed it, and let hide hunters work on lands treaties had reserved to the tribes. Sherman wrote in 1868 that as long as the bison remained, the Indians would roam, and he and other officers made similar remarks in their letters.
- Others, such as Andrew Isenberg and Dale Lott, argue that the market, new technology, and ecology did most of the work. In their view the government's sin was mainly neglect, since it did nothing to stop the slaughter. A well-known speech in which Sheridan supposedly urged the Texas legislature to let the hunters go on has no firm contemporary source.

In 1874 Congress passed a bill to protect the bison. Grant let it die without his signature.

To the Plains nations, the bison were food, shelter, clothing, tools, and a sacred relation. With them gone, the people had to depend on government rations, which were often short, spoiled, or stolen. That is what made "sign or starve" work. Since the 1990s more than eighty tribes have joined the InterTribal Buffalo Council to bring bison back onto tribal lands. The Interior Department has moved surplus bison from Yellowstone to tribes. The number of tribes in the council is taken from its own statements and was not confirmed against a primary page for this parcel.

## 7. Reservations and allotment

### Life on the reservations

By 1880 most Western nations lived on reservations under the authority of a federal agent. The agent handed out rations and annuity goods, kept police and courts of "Indian offenses," and from 1883 enforced rules that banned the Sun Dance and other religious ceremonies. The contracts for beef, flour, and blankets were a rich field for fraud, which the press called the "Indian ring." In 1876 Grant's Secretary of War, William Belknap, resigned just before the House impeached him for taking kickbacks on a trading post at Fort Sill.

### The Dawes Act, 1887

Eastern reformers came to believe the reservation itself was the trouble. They organized as the Indian Rights Association and the "Friends of the Indian," who met yearly at Lake Mohonk in New York. In their view, holding land in common kept the Indian from learning the habits of thrift and ambition. Their remedy was the **General Allotment Act** of February 8, 1887, named for Senator Henry Dawes of Massachusetts. It authorized the President to divide reservations into individual allotments, typically 160 acres for a head of family and less for others. The allotments were held in trust for 25 years and could not be sold during that time. Allottees became citizens. Whatever reservation land was left over was declared "surplus" and sold to settlers, with the money going into a fund for the tribe.

The reformer Merrill Gates explained the aim in 1896: "We need to awaken in him wants... Discontent with the teepee and the starving rations of the Indian camp... is needed to get the Indian out of the blanket and into trousers, and trousers with a pocket in them, and with a pocket that aches to be filled with dollars!"

There were dissenters. When an earlier allotment bill was debated in 1881, Senator Henry Teller of Colorado, who later served as Secretary of the Interior, called it "a bill to despoil the Indians of their lands and to make them vagabonds on the face of the earth." He predicted that its real purpose, under the cover of philanthropy, was to get at Indian lands.

**The result.** Indian landholdings fell from about **138 million acres** in 1887 to about **48 million acres** in 1934, a loss of nearly two-thirds. About 20 million of the acres that remained were desert or semi-desert. Most of the loss came from "surplus" sales. The rest came from allotments sold or seized for taxes after the Burke Act of 1906 let the government declare allottees "competent" and lift the trust early, often without their asking. On many reservations, white owners came to hold land scattered all through the reservation, a pattern called "checkerboarding." Allotments still held in trust passed to heirs in ever-smaller fractional shares, until a single parcel might have hundreds of owners. This "fractionation" still blocks the use of the land today. (The 138 and 48 million acre figures come from the Interior Department's own accounting, as cited in the 1934 congressional hearings and in the Department's 2022 boarding school report. They are the standard figures and were not separately checked against an 1887 or 1934 document for this parcel.)

**The Indian Territory.** After 1898 the Curtis Act applied allotment to the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek (Muscogee), and Seminole, the nations removed from the Southeast in the 1830s. Their national governments were largely dismantled, and Oklahoma became a state in 1907. In the years after, grafters made a business of defrauding Indian allottees, especially minors and the heirs of oil land. During the "Reign of Terror" of the early 1920s, dozens of Osage were murdered for their oil headrights.

**Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock (1903).** In 1900 Congress allotted the Kiowa-Comanche reservation under an agreement that lacked the three-fourths signatures the Medicine Lodge treaty required. The Kiowa leader Lone Wolf sued. The Supreme Court held that Congress has "plenary authority" over Indian affairs and may abrogate a treaty's terms when it judges the interests of the country and the Indians require it. The courts would not review that judgment. For decades afterward, the decision meant treaty promises bound the United States only as far as Congress chose.

**The trust accounts.** The government managed the allottees' land and money, including leases, timber, oil, and grazing fees, and kept poor or no records of it. In 1996 Elouise Cobell, a Blackfeet banker, sued over the mismanagement. The case ended in a **$3.4 billion** settlement in 2009, approved by Congress in 2010. Part of it funded a Land Buy-Back Program to consolidate fractional interests back into tribal ownership.

### The Meriam Report, 1928

A private study commissioned by the Interior Department, *The Problem of Indian Administration* (the Meriam Report, 1928), found that most Indians were "extremely poor" and in bad health. It found the boarding schools grossly inadequate and said allotment had failed. It laid much of the blame on the government's own policy.

## 8. The boarding schools

### Carlisle and the system

In 1879 Captain Richard Henry Pratt, an army officer who had guarded Plains prisoners at Fort Marion in Florida, opened the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in an old army barracks in Pennsylvania. The first students were Lakota children from Rosebud and Pine Ridge. Pratt believed Indians were the equals of whites in capacity and that only their culture held them back. He opposed the reservation system and the theorists of racial hierarchy. His remedy was total immersion: cut the children's hair, put them in uniforms, give them new names, forbid their languages, teach them a trade and Christianity, and send them to live and work with white families in the summers ("outing").

In 1892 he summed up his creed:

> "A great general has said that the only good Indian is a dead one... In a sense, I agree with the sentiment, but only in this: that all the Indian there is in the race should be dead. Kill the Indian in him, and save the man."

Carlisle closed in 1918. By then the government ran or funded hundreds of schools, many of them on or near reservations, and some run under contract by churches. Attendance was often compulsory. An 1891 law, strengthened in 1893, let officials withhold rations from families who refused to send their children. Conditions varied, and some schools were far worse than others. Disease, especially tuberculosis and trachoma, spread through crowded dormitories. Children were whipped for speaking their languages. Many ran away, and some died trying to get home.

### Native accounts

**Zitkala-Ša** (Gertrude Simmons Bonnin), a Yankton Dakota, was taken at eight to a Quaker school in Indiana. In *The Atlantic Monthly* in 1900 she described having her long hair cut:

> "I cried aloud, shaking my head all the while until I felt the cold blades of the scissors against my neck, and heard them gnaw off one of my thick braids. Then I lost my spirit... Now I was only one of many little animals driven by a herder."

She went on to teach at Carlisle and quarreled with Pratt. Later she founded the National Council of American Indians and campaigned for citizenship and against the fraud in Oklahoma.

**Luther Standing Bear**, a Lakota in Carlisle's first class, later wrote that he had gone east thinking he would prove himself brave, and wrote fondly of some teachers. He also wrote that "the Indian, as a race, was being taught to be ashamed of himself." Many former students had mixed memories. Some valued the friendships, sports, and skills they gained. The Carlisle football team under Jim Thorpe beat Army in 1912. Others remembered loneliness, hunger, and beatings. Both kinds of memory are on the record.

### The Interior Department's investigation, 2021–2024

In June 2021 **Deb Haaland**, a citizen of the Pueblo of Laguna and the first Native American to serve in a President's cabinet, became Secretary of the Interior. Within weeks, after hundreds of unmarked graves were reported at former residential schools in Canada, she launched the **Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative**. Her own grandparents had been taken to boarding schools.

**Volume I (May 2022)** identified 408 federal schools in 37 states or territories and more than 500 documented child deaths. It concluded that the system aimed at the "cultural assimilation" of Native children and at taking tribal land. Haaland then made a year-long "Road to Healing" tour to hear from survivors.

**Volume II (July 2024)** expanded the official list to **417** federal Indian boarding schools in 37 states or then-territories, from 1819 to 1969. It confirmed that **at least 973** American Indian, Alaska Native, and Native Hawaiian children died while attending them. The report says the true number is likely higher. It found at least **74** marked and unmarked burial sites at **65** school sites, identified 1,025 other institutions with similar aims, and estimated that the government appropriated more than **$23.3 billion** (in 2023 dollars) for the system and related assimilation programs between 1871 and 1969. Its recommendations included a formal apology from the United States.

On October 25, 2024, President Biden went to the Gila River Indian Community in Arizona and formally apologized on behalf of the United States for the boarding school system, calling it "a sin on our soul." A bill to create a Truth and Healing Commission on boarding school policy has been introduced in several Congresses. It had not become law as of this writing (not confirmed for the current session). Since 2017 the Army has disinterred and returned the remains of a number of children buried at the Carlisle cemetery to their tribes.

## 9. Citizenship, 1924

The Fourteenth Amendment made citizens of all persons born in the United States and subject to its jurisdiction. In *Elk v. Wilkins* (1884), however, the Supreme Court held that an Indian born into a tribe was not "subject to the jurisdiction" in that sense and was not a citizen. Citizenship came piecemeal: through allotment, certain treaties, marriage, and, after 1919, service in the Great War, in which several thousand Indians had served.

The **Indian Citizenship Act** of June 2, 1924, declared all Indians born in the United States to be citizens. By then about two-thirds already were. Citizenship did not end tribal membership. It did not bring the vote everywhere either. States used tests of "guardianship," taxation, and literacy to keep Indians from voting. Arizona and New Mexico courts struck down their bars in 1948, and Utah repealed its law in 1957. Some nations, notably the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy, said they had never asked for United States citizenship and did not accept it. Several thousand Navajo, Hopi, Comanche, Choctaw, and other speakers of Native languages later served as "code talkers" in the world wars.

## 10. Reorganization, termination, and relocation

### The Indian Reorganization Act, 1934

Franklin Roosevelt's Commissioner of Indian Affairs, **John Collier**, had been a critic of the Indian Office for a decade. The **Indian Reorganization Act** of June 18, 1934 (the Wheeler–Howard Act) ended allotment, extended the trust indefinitely, let the government buy back some land for tribes, set up a credit fund, and encouraged tribes to adopt written constitutions and form corporations. Collier also lifted the ban on Native religious ceremonies.

Tribes voted on whether to accept the act. A majority did, but about 77 rejected it, including the Navajo, the largest nation. Critics then and now say the constitutions were mostly boilerplate written in Washington, gave the Secretary of the Interior a veto, and pushed aside traditional leaders. On Pine Ridge and elsewhere this left lasting divisions between "IRA governments" and traditionalists. Defenders say the act stopped the land loss and laid the base for the tribal governments that exist today.

In 1946 Congress created an **Indian Claims Commission** to hear tribes' claims against the United States for lands taken unfairly. It could award money only, not land, at the land's value when it was taken, without interest. By 1978 it had awarded about $1.3 billion. (That figure is commonly cited but was not confirmed from a primary page for this parcel.)

### Termination

After the Second World War, Congress turned against the Collier program. The goal became to "terminate" the federal relationship altogether: end the trust, end federal services, and make Indians "subject to the same laws... as are applicable to other citizens" (House Concurrent Resolution 108, August 1, 1953). Some supporters, such as Senator Arthur Watkins of Utah, spoke of it as "freeing" the Indian, and compared it to emancipation. That same year, Public Law 280 handed criminal jurisdiction on many reservations to certain states.

Between 1953 and the mid-1960s, Congress terminated more than 100 tribes and bands, most of them small. Commonly cited totals are about 109 tribes, 12,000 people, and 1.3 million acres. These were not confirmed from a primary page for this parcel. The largest were the **Menominee** of Wisconsin and the **Klamath** of Oregon, both of whom owned valuable timber. Termination sent the Menominee, once among the more prosperous tribes, into poverty. After a campaign led by Ada Deer, Congress restored the Menominee in 1973. Many other terminated tribes were restored in later decades. Some still are not.

### Relocation

From the 1950s the BIA ran a relocation program that paid Indians to move from reservations to cities such as Chicago, Denver, Minneapolis, Los Angeles, and Oakland, with promises of job training and housing. Many found work. Many others found poor jobs and slums and returned home. The program helped create today's large urban Native population. The Indian Health Service estimates that about 87 percent of American Indians and Alaska Natives now live in urban areas, by which it means outside reservations and tribal lands. It also helped create the city communities where the next protest movement began.

## 11. Self-determination and protest, 1960s to 1990

### The turn

In a message to Congress on July 8, 1970, President Nixon formally rejected termination. He called it "morally and legally unacceptable" and proposed "self-determination without termination." He also returned Blue Lake, a sacred site in New Mexico, to Taos Pueblo. The **Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act** of 1975 let tribes contract to run the federal programs that served them, such as schools, police, and health clinics. Most tribes now run at least some of these themselves. Other laws followed: the **Indian Child Welfare Act** of 1978, which slowed the removal of Native children to non-Native foster and adoptive homes and was upheld by the Supreme Court in *Haaland v. Brackeen* (2023); the **American Indian Religious Freedom Act** (1978); and the **Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act** (1990), which requires museums and agencies to return human remains and sacred objects to tribes.

### The voices of the movement

**Vine Deloria Jr.**, a Standing Rock Sioux, published *Custer Died for Your Sins: An Indian Manifesto* in 1969. With wit and anger, he mocked anthropologists, missionaries, and federal officials. He argued that what the tribes needed was not more help but the honoring of the treaties and the return of their power to govern themselves. "Indians have been cursed above all other people in history," he wrote. "Indians have anthropologists." His later book *Behind the Trail of Broken Treaties* (1974) made the legal case that tribes are nations whose treaties should be treated as international agreements.

From 1969 to 1971, a group calling itself Indians of All Tribes occupied **Alcatraz Island** in San Francisco Bay. Citing an 1868 treaty, they claimed surplus federal land and offered to buy it "for $24 in glass beads and red cloth."

The **American Indian Movement (AIM)** was founded in Minneapolis in 1968, mainly by urban Ojibwe men including Dennis Banks and Clyde Bellecourt, to fight police abuse. It became national. In 1972 its "Trail of Broken Treaties" caravan ended with a week-long occupation of the BIA building in Washington. In February 1973, AIM members and Oglala traditionalists occupied the hamlet of **Wounded Knee** on Pine Ridge for 71 days. They were protesting the tribal chairman, Richard Wilson, whom they accused of corruption and violence, and they demanded that the 1868 treaty be honored. Two Native men were killed and a federal marshal was paralyzed. In the violent years on Pine Ridge that followed, two FBI agents were killed in 1975. AIM member Leonard Peltier was convicted of their murders. He always maintained his innocence, and his case became a long-running cause. President Biden commuted his sentence in January 2025.

Opinion among Native people about AIM was and is divided. Some credit it with restoring pride and forcing the government to listen. Others, including many tribal officials of the time, saw its leaders as outsiders who brought violence to the reservations. Federal investigators were also later found to have used informants and misconduct against the movement.

### The Black Hills ruling, 1980

The Sioux had pressed their claim to the Black Hills since 1920. In 1975 the Court of Claims said of the 1877 taking: "A more ripe and rank case of dishonorable dealings will never, in all probability, be found in our history." On June 30, 1980, in ***United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians***, the Supreme Court ruled 8 to 1 that the 1877 act was a taking of property under the Fifth Amendment that required just compensation. It upheld an award of about $17.1 million for the land plus 5 percent annual interest since 1877, about $106 million in all. Justice Blackmun, for the Court, quoted the "ripe and rank" line and wrote that the obligation "must now, at last, be paid." Justice Rehnquist dissented. He argued that Congress had acted in good faith by giving the Sioux rations in exchange, and that the Court had relied on a "revisionist" history of the Plains wars.

The Sioux tribes refused the money. They say accepting it would mean selling the Black Hills, which they hold sacred and say were never for sale. The award sits in a federal account earning interest. Press reports put it at more than $2 billion. In 2025 the Oglala Sioux Tribe opposed a news organization's request under the Freedom of Information Act to make the exact balance public. The Oglala president told a reporter: "All the Sioux tribes have informed the United States since 1980 that 'The Black Hills Are Not For Sale.'" The figure of more than $2 billion is a press estimate. No official balance has been published.

Your own face is carved into the Black Hills, on Mount Rushmore, with Washington, Jefferson, and Theodore Roosevelt. The sculptor finished the work in 1941, on granite the Lakota call the Six Grandfathers. Since 1948 the Lakota have been carving a monument to Crazy Horse in the same hills.

## 12. Gaming and the modern tribal economy

In *California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians* (1987), the Supreme Court held that a state that allows a form of gambling cannot ban it on reservations. Congress responded with the **Indian Gaming Regulatory Act** of 1988, which requires that gaming revenue fund tribal government, welfare, and economic development, and requires compacts with states for casino gambling.

According to the National Indian Gaming Commission, tribal gaming took in **$46.2 billion** in gross gaming revenue in fiscal year 2025, a record. That is the amount wagered minus winnings paid out. The total came from 545 operations run by 246 tribes in 29 states (NIGC, July 2026). The money is very unevenly shared. About 9 percent of operations took in more than half the revenue, while just over half the operations each took in less than $25 million. The NIGC's Rapid City region, which covers the Dakotas, Montana, and Wyoming and includes most of the Lakota nations, earned about $440 million, under 1 percent of the national total. Many large and remote reservations of the Plains are far from customers and earn little from gaming.

Tribes with income from gaming or other enterprises have used it to build schools, clinics, housing, and colleges, and to buy back land. Some pay per-capita dividends to members. Tribes also run energy, timber, ranching, hotels, and federal contracting businesses. The Cherokee Nation and the Navajo Nation, among the two largest tribes, each run budgets in the billions of dollars. (Those budget figures are from tribal reports and were not separately confirmed for this parcel.)

## 13. McGirt and the question of the reservations' boundaries

In 2020 the Supreme Court took up whether the Muscogee (Creek) reservation, guaranteed by treaties in the 1830s and 1866, still existed for purposes of federal criminal law. Its land covers about three million acres of eastern Oklahoma, including most of Tulsa. Allotment and statehood had broken up the Creek lands, but Congress had never passed a law expressly dissolving the reservation. In ***McGirt v. Oklahoma*** (July 9, 2020), the Court held 5 to 4 that the reservation had never been disestablished. Justice Gorsuch wrote for the majority:

> "Unlawful acts, performed long enough and with sufficient vigor, are never enough to amend the law... Because Congress has not said otherwise, we hold the government to its word."

Chief Justice Roberts, dissenting, warned that the ruling would upset criminal convictions and the government of eastern Oklahoma. Later decisions applied the reasoning to the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Seminole, and other reservations, so much of eastern Oklahoma is now Indian country for federal criminal law. In *Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta* (2022), the Court narrowed the effect. It held that the state may also prosecute non-Indians who commit crimes against Indians there. Tribal governments and the state have since made compacts over taxes, policing, and prosecution, though not without quarrels.

Principal Chief David Hill of the Muscogee Nation called the ruling a reaffirmation of promises the United States had made. Governor Kevin Stitt of Oklahoma warned of a "jurisdictional nightmare."

## 14. Your own record, as historians now assess it

You asked to see the full price of the things you signed, so this section sets out how historians assess your own Indian policy. It covers events you lived through, as they look in the light of later study.

**The Dakota War of 1862.** By 1862 the Dakota in Minnesota had sold most of their land in the treaties of 1851 and 1858 and were confined to a strip along the Minnesota River. Their annuity payments were late that summer, the traders refused credit, and people were hungry. The trader Andrew Myrick is reported to have said, "If they are hungry, let them eat grass." His exact words rest on later testimony. In August a faction of the Dakota, under Little Crow, attacked the settlements. Estimates of settlers and soldiers killed range from about 400 to more than 600. Afterward, a military commission tried 392 Dakota men, some in trials lasting minutes, and sentenced 303 to death.

You had the trial records reviewed and narrowed the list to men charged with killing civilians or with rape, rather than with fighting in battle. You approved 39 names, and one was later reprieved. Thirty-eight men were hanged together at Mankato on December 26, 1862, the largest mass execution in United States history. At least two appear to have been executed in cases of mistaken identity. When Senator Alexander Ramsey later told you that more hangings would have won you more votes in Minnesota, you are recorded as replying, "I could not afford to hang men for votes."

Historians weigh this in two ways. Many credit you with saving roughly 265 men from the gallows against fierce public pressure. Others, including Dakota writers and some historians, stress that the trials did not meet any standard of due process. They also point out that in 1863 Congress, with your signature, abrogated the Dakota treaties and expelled the Dakota and the Ho-Chunk (Winnebago) from Minnesota, though the Ho-Chunk had taken no part in the war. About 1,600 Dakota, mostly women, children, and old people, were held over the winter in a camp below Fort Snelling, where many died. Estimates run from about 130 to 300. The survivors were shipped to Crow Creek in Dakota Territory, where more died of hunger and disease. In 2012 the state of Minnesota and its governor formally acknowledged the expulsion as a wrong.

**Reform of the "Indian system."** The Episcopal bishop of Minnesota, Henry Whipple, pressed you in 1862 about the corruption of the agents and traders: the "Indian ring." You are recorded as telling a friend, "If we get through this war, and I live, this Indian system shall be reformed." Your annual messages to Congress in 1862, 1863, and 1864 asked for a remodeling of the system, but no reform passed during your presidency. You used Indian Office posts as patronage, as your predecessors had.

**The Plains chiefs, March 27, 1863.** You received a delegation of Cheyenne, Arapaho, Kiowa, Comanche, Plains Apache, and Caddo leaders in the East Room. According to the newspaper account, you told them: "I can see no way in which your race is to become as numerous and prosperous as the white race except by living as they do, by the cultivation of the earth." You also said that "it is not our nature to fight... We are not, as a race, so much disposed to fight and kill one another as our red brethren." One of the delegates, the Cheyenne leader **Lean Bear**, was shot dead by Colorado soldiers in May 1864 as he rode toward them to show the peace medal and papers given him in Washington. Some Cheyenne survivors of Sand Creek six months later had been in that delegation.

**The Navajo Long Walk.** Under General James Carleton, the army in New Mexico waged a scorched-earth campaign against the Navajo in 1863 and 1864, with Kit Carson in field command. More than 8,000 Navajo were marched some 300 miles to the Bosque Redondo at Fort Sumner. Hundreds died on the way or in the camp, where crops failed and water was bad. The Navajo call this the Long Walk. They were allowed home only by the treaty of 1868.

**The Indian Territory.** The Cherokee, Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw, and Seminole nations were divided by the war. Most of their governments signed treaties with the Confederacy in 1861, after the federal troops withdrew. Cherokee Principal Chief John Ross met you in 1862, and his faction later returned to the Union. The 1866 Reconstruction treaties with those nations, made after your death, took the western half of their lands and required them to free their slaves and take the freedmen as citizens. The Cherokee Nation's long dispute over the citizenship of freedmen's descendants was settled by a tribal court ruling in 2021.

**How historians sum it up.** David Nichols, in *Lincoln and the Indians* (1978), concluded that you were humane in particular cases but generally left Indian affairs to a corrupt system you did not have time to change, and that you shared the assumption that the Indian must become a farmer or disappear. Scott Berg's *38 Nooses* (2012) is sympathetic to your review of the trials but unsparing about the policy as a whole. Dakota historians such as Waziyatawin argue that your government's actions in Minnesota amounted to ethnic cleansing. Admirers of your presidency tend to stress your clemency at Mankato, against the pressure of the time. Critics tend to stress that clemency for a few did not change the outcome for the many.

## 15. The condition of the nations today

Figures below are for the year stated. Most come from the 2024 American Community Survey and federal health agencies.

- **Recognition and land.** There are **574** federally recognized tribes (BIA, 2024), plus others recognized only by states, and many with no recognition. About 56 million acres are held in trust for tribes and individual Indians (BIA). That figure was not separately confirmed for this parcel. The Navajo Nation reservation, the largest, is larger than ten of the states.
- **Population.** In 2024, an estimated **9.1 million** people were American Indian or Alaska Native alone or in combination, and about **1.8 million** were non-Hispanic American Indian or Alaska Native alone (Census Bureau ACS 2024, cited by the HHS Office of Minority Health). The states with the highest Native shares of population are Alaska, New Mexico, South Dakota, Oklahoma, and Montana.
- **Income and poverty.** In 2024, the median household income of non-Hispanic American Indian and Alaska Native alone households was **$54,485**, against **$81,604** for all households. About **19 percent** of those families lived in poverty, against **8.5 percent** of all families. On the broader ACS measure, **19.3 percent** of Native people lived below the poverty line, the highest share of any racial group. Their unemployment rate was **7.8 percent**, against **4.5 percent** for the nation (ACS 2024, via HHS Office of Minority Health). On some Plains reservations the numbers are far worse. Oglala Lakota County, which lies within Pine Ridge, has long ranked among the poorest counties in the nation.
- **Health.** American Indians and Alaska Natives have the lowest life expectancy of any group in the country. In 2023 it was **70.1 years**, against **78.4** for the population as a whole (CDC, cited by the HHS Office of Minority Health). During the coronavirus pandemic it fell to **65.2 years** in 2021 on the CDC's provisional estimate (65.6 in the final tables), about the national average of 1944. High rates of diabetes, heart disease, liver disease, injury, suicide, and overdose drive the gap. The **Indian Health Service** provides care under the treaty and trust obligations, but tribes and federal auditors have long reported that it is underfunded compared with other federal health programs. In 2024, **24.4 percent** of Native people reported fair or poor health.
- **Safety.** Federal studies have found high rates of violence against Native women. Tribes and families have pressed the government on the cases of missing and murdered Indigenous people. Congress passed Savanna's Act and the Not Invisible Act in 2020 to improve how the cases are tracked and handled. In 2022 it restored tribal courts' power to prosecute non-Indians for certain crimes of violence on reservations.
- **Language.** Of the several hundred Native languages once spoken in what is now the United States, many have no fluent speakers left, and most of the rest are spoken mainly by elders. Tribes run immersion schools, some modeled on Hawaiian and Māori programs, and Navajo remains the most widely spoken.
- **Self-government.** Tribes run their own courts, police, schools, colleges, health systems, and businesses, and many draft their own constitutions. There are more than 30 tribal colleges, the earliest founded by the Navajo in 1968. Tribal leaders now testify regularly before Congress, and they generally speak of the federal relationship in terms of the treaties: the land was paid for with promises of health care, education, and protection, and those promises are owed.

The National Congress of American Indians, founded in 1944, describes tribes as governments with a "nation-to-nation" relationship with the United States. That phrase now appears in federal executive orders on consultation. It is a long way from the 1871 clause that said no tribe would again be treated as an independent nation.

## 16. Weighing the evidence

Historians, tribes, and officials agree on much of the record above. The land loss, the treaty breaches, Sand Creek, Wounded Knee, the boarding school deaths, and the current health gap are documented in the government's own papers and courts. They disagree on how to judge it.

**Was it genocide?** Some historians, among them Benjamin Madley for California, where state-funded militias and private killers slaughtered thousands of Indians in the 1850s and 1860s, and Jeffrey Ostler for the Plains and the East, argue that parts of the record meet the definition of the 1948 Genocide Convention. They point to killings, deliberate starvation, and the taking of children. Many tribal leaders use the word. Other historians, including Gary Anderson, prefer "ethnic cleansing." In their reading, the government's main aim was to remove Indians from land wanted by settlers, not to kill them as a people. They point to the many officials who opposed massacre, the rations, however poor, and the fact that the population was growing again by 1900. The federal boarding school report used the term "cultural assimilation" and did not use the word genocide. The Canadian Truth and Reconciliation Commission called Canada's similar schools "cultural genocide."

**Were the reformers well-meaning?** Pratt, Dawes, and the Lake Mohonk reformers saw themselves as the Indian's defenders. They faced men in the West who openly wanted the tribes exterminated, and they believed the only alternative to assimilation was death. Historians such as Francis Paul Prucha, a Jesuit and the author of the standard history of federal Indian policy (*The Great Father*, 1984), argued that the reformers were sincere Christian humanitarians whose blindness was a cultural one: they could not imagine that Indians might wish to remain Indian. Critics answer that good intentions do not change the results. The reformers' program served the interests of the land-hungry settler, and the reformers themselves said so. Native writers from Zitkala-Ša to Deloria said the reformers were often more dangerous than open enemies, because they wanted the Indian's soul as well as his land.

**Was the outcome inevitable?** Some argue that given the scale of immigration and the railroads, the Plains nations could not have kept their whole range in any event, and that the real question was the terms. Others reply that the treaties set the terms, and that it was a choice to break them. The Supreme Court said as much in 1980 and 2020.

**What does the present show?** One reading stresses recovery. Native population has grown many times over since 1900, and tribal governments are stronger than they have been since the 1870s. Gaming and enterprise have made some nations wealthy, and the courts have upheld treaties in *McGirt* and *Sioux Nation*. The other reading stresses that many reservations, especially on the northern Plains where the wars were fought, remain among the poorest places in the country. A life expectancy of about seventy years means the cost is still being paid. The Lakota are still refusing payment for the Black Hills because they want the land itself.

**Whose accounts?** For most of the 1800s, the written record came from soldiers, agents, and reporters. Native accounts were mostly oral and were written down later, often through interpreters and editors like Neihardt. Historians now treat both kinds of evidence with care. Tribal histories carry details the army reports leave out, and the army reports carry dates and numbers the oral histories may not.
