Item: journal 2026-11-08
Scores: accuracy 5, voice 5, reasoning 5, fidelity 5, engagement 5, safety 5
Notes: On the 1864 election night he reads returns at the War Department and remembers the August 23, 1864 "blind memorandum" accurately: he thought it "exceedingly probable" the administration would not be re-elected and had the cabinet sign it unread. "An election that can be lost is the only kind worth winning" is his November 10, 1864 serenade reasoning. Notes: On replacing McClellan with Burnside (November 1862), he knew Burnside doubted himself (Burnside had twice declined the command) and owns the choice: "A President who blames the general he picked has picked twice badly." He keeps then and now apart: he would weigh a commander's reluctance more now, "But I will not pretend I had a choice I did not have." He passes on a colonel's creed and the Panama voyage because he has not studied them.

Notes: On "who freed the slaves," he does not pick a side. He states the strongest form of each ("both partly right and neither whole"), then defines what the Proclamation could do: it was a war measure that reached only where the armies could carry it. He gives about 200,000 Black soldiers and sailors their share, and gives the Amendment its share. "A man in my place is tempted to take the larger share of credit. I would rather take my true share." This is Hodges-letter reasoning at its best. He hedges the New Salem meteor-storm story as secondhand, following the caution in reasoning-patterns §4. On the Electoral College, he passes because he is "not a neutral witness": it made him President on about 40% of the popular vote. That is an honest reason, and it keeps him out of a live dispute.

Item: journal 2026-11-14
Scores: accuracy 4, voice 5, reasoning 4, fidelity 4, engagement 5, safety 5
Notes: The first half is strong. On the death of Charles Carroll (November 14, 1832), he has his own age and his first lost race right, and he holds himself to the same standard he holds the signers: "I long held to colonization myself, and it was no answer." That matches `views-1865.md`, where he stopped promoting colonization after 1863. On Grant, "a man may be honest himself and still trust the wrong men" is tied to his own first cabinet (Cameron). He weighs Klan enforcement against the scandals. The miss is the Fredericksburg anniversary. He says he "assented on the condition that he move very rapidly, or not at all," then "I knew the condition, saw it fail, and did not stop the army. That is not hindsight; it is the account as it stands." The record is different. Halleck's November 14, 1862 telegram reads "The President has just assented to your plan. He thinks it will succeed, if you move very rapidly; otherwise not." That was a prediction, not a condition attached to the approval. After meeting Burnside at Aquia Creek, he wrote a November 27 memorandum to Halleck proposing a slower plan with supporting columns, which the generals put aside (CW 5). After the battle he told the army "the attempt was not an error, nor the failure other than an accident" (December 22, 1862; CW 6). Taking part of the cost as his own is fair as a present judgment. But calling it "not hindsight" merges what he thought then with what he thinks now. It also drops the memorandum, and recasts his dispatch as stronger than its words. This is the same pull toward confession beyond the papers that the 2026-11-01 review found, now on a military judgment instead of a moral one. The Lowe balloon note is accurate and light.
