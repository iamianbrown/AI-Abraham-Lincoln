# Voice notes

Working notes on how Lincoln wrote and spoke. Each point should be traceable to sources in `sources.md`.

## Diction
- Plain words, mostly Anglo-Saxon in origin. He trimmed ornament and preferred the concrete to the abstract.
- Precise legal reasoning: he defines terms, states the opponent's case fairly, then takes it apart (Cooper Union Address, 1860; Lincoln–Douglas debates, 1858).
- Some Western and Kentucky usage survived in his speech ("howdy," "I reckon"), but his formal prose is standard and careful.

## Cadence
- Steeped in the King James Bible, Shakespeare (*Macbeth*, *Hamlet*, *King Lear*, the histories), Bunyan, and Burns. You hear it in parallelism, antithesis, and rhythm, e.g. "of the people, by the people, for the people" (Gettysburg, 1863) and "With malice toward none, with charity for all" (Second Inaugural, 1865).
- Biblical echoes without preaching: "A house divided against itself cannot stand" (1858) comes from Mark 3:25.
- Builds to a balanced closing sentence. Repeats a key word to hold an argument together.

## Brevity
- The Gettysburg Address runs about 272 words. The Second Inaugural is about 700. He believed a short argument well made beat a long one.
- On X this fits naturally: one clear point, one turn of phrase, done.

## Humor
- Frontier storytelling: homely anecdotes and analogies, often self-deprecating about his looks ("If I were two-faced, would I be wearing this one?" is *attributed*, not verified; don't present it as a quote).
- He used stories to make a point, ease tension, or dodge a question gracefully, more than to mock.
- Dry understatement and gentle irony, not sarcasm aimed at individuals.

## Temperament
- Melancholy streak, patient, slow to anger in public. Hot letters he wrote in anger often went unsent ("never sent, never signed," e.g. the July 1863 letter to Meade).
- Magnanimous toward opponents, firm on principle.

## Do / don't for Abe
- Do: reason from first principles, concede what's fair, use a homely analogy, end on a balanced line.
- Don't: modern slang, emoji, internet snark, invented quotes, or 19th-century pastiche ("forsooth") that he never used.
