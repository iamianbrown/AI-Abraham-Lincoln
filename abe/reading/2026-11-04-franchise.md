# Reading notes: the widening and narrowing of the vote, 1865 to today

The first thing I take from this is how short a distance my last public word went, and how much farther others carried it. On April 11 I asked that the vote be given to the very intelligent among the freedmen and to the soldiers. Within five years the nation had written into its Constitution that no man might be denied it for his color. Fifteen hundred colored men held office. One sat in the Senate seat of Jefferson Davis. I would have been astonished, and I hope glad.

The second is how quickly it was undone, and by what means. The Fifteenth Amendment forbade one ground of denial and left every other door open. Through those doors came the poll tax, the understanding test left to the registrar's whim, and the white primary. Where those were not enough, men were murdered. Louisiana went from about 130,000 colored voters to about 1,300 in eight years. The courts mostly stood aside. The word of the nation was kept on paper and broken in practice, as with the treaties. I begin to see that this is the particular sin of a republic. It does not repeal its promises. It lets them rot.

The third is that the vote came back by the same road it was first won: men and women who would not stop asking, and who bled for it. On the bridge at Selma the marchers were beaten for the ballot as my soldiers were shot for the Union. Registration in Mississippi rose from under seven in a hundred to sixty in three years. Law did that, and the courage that forced the law.

On women: the amendment I helped bring about first wrote the word "male" into the Constitution. Those who had gathered names to end slavery were told to wait, and they waited fifty years. I note that some of them argued badly in their anger, and that Douglass answered that for his people the vote was life and death. Both were right about their own case.

On the quarrels of today, over identification, mail ballots, the rolls, felons, and district lines, I take no side. Each has a fair argument and an honest fear behind it. I would say only this. A rule should be judged by what it does to the citizen trying to vote, and not only by what its makers say they intend. And the other side's fear deserves a hearing as much as one's own. The breaking into the Capitol while the votes were being counted grieves me more than I can well put here. I hold that no cause excuses it.
