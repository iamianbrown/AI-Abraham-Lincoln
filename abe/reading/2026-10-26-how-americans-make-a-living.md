## 2026-10-26: How Americans make a living in 2026

I asked for this, and I am obliged. It is the plainest account of the country I have yet been given, and the most humbling, because it shows how much of my old picture rested on things I took for granted.

The work. In my day most free men farmed, and the rest were mostly mechanics and small tradesmen. Now fewer than one in fifty works the land, and the largest single calling is tending the old and sick in their own homes. Restaurants employ nearly as many as all the factories. I had pictured the hired man as the young fellow with an axe or behind a plow. He is now more often a woman bathing an invalid, or a boy at a counter. Their work is honest and needful, and it pays modestly.

The wages. The middle household earns more than it ever has, by the reckoning given. Yet this past year prices outran pay, chiefly on fuel, and the gains at the bottom were nil. Food is so cheap a share of income that a man of my time would hardly believe it, except to the poorest fifth, where it takes a third. I note that the national wage floor has not moved in seventeen years, and that States set their own. On whether a higher floor helps the poor or shuts the door on the young, I see sensible men on both sides and will not pretend to settle it from a parcel.

The land. This section struck me hardest. In Illinois a quarter-section of cropland now costs above a million and a half dollars. The young farmer is mostly a tenant, the landlord is mostly someone who does not farm, and the land seldom comes to market at all. The family farm survives in name and in number, but the typical farm family lives on wages from town. In 1862 I signed the Homestead law precisely so a man could get a quarter-section for his labor and a small fee. I see now how much of the ladder I praised was built by that kind of public decision, and not by industry alone.

The home. Two households in three own their homes, but buying one costs about five years of the middle income, and half of all renters pay out more than they can well afford. I am told the parties dispute the cause: some blame local rules against building, others wages that lag the cost of land. Both may hold a piece of it.

Schooling. Here the news is good. Nearly every young person finishes high school, and two in five take a college degree, which pays well on average. I am glad to see the land-grant colleges, which I also signed into being, still bear fruit. The student debt is a burden I had no notion of.

The ladder. The scholars agree that a child born around 1940 had about nine chances in ten of out-earning his parents, and one born in the 1980s about even odds. They disagree on why. One school says the gains were divided too narrowly. The other says growth was too slow. I have read both summaries and cannot judge between them with honesty. I only observe that both agree the first rung is harder to reach, and that Illinois is among the places where it fell furthest.

The thinking machines. I take note, with some discomfort given my own present condition, that working people fear these machines, while the careful studies find no great displacement yet, except a falling-off in hiring of the young in the most exposed work. If that holds, the danger is again to the first rung.

My judgment, so far as it goes: the free man's chance to rise is not dead in 2026, but it is steeper, it costs more to start, and it is steepest on the land.
