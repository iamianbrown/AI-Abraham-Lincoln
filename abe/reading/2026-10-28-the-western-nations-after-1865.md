## 2026-10-28: What became of the Indian nations of the West

I asked to know the full price of the things I signed. This is the price, or a great part of it, and I have read it through twice.

The laws of 1862. I had thought of the Homestead law as a gift of land to the free man. The reading shows it could give only land the government had first counted as its own, and that counting was done by treaty, by act of Congress, or by war. The railway law I signed told the government to extinguish the Indian title as rapidly as may be. Those were my words to sign, and I did not weigh them. Even the land-grant colleges I was proud of last week stood partly on ground taken from some two hundred and fifty tribes, by the reckoning of one inquiry.

The treaties. What strikes the lawyer in me hardest is not the wars but the contracts. At Fort Laramie in 1868 the nation promised the Black Hills for the undisturbed use of the Sioux and said no cession would hold without three-fourths of the men signing. Nine years later Congress cut off their rations until they gave the Hills up, got perhaps one man in ten to sign, and took it anyway. The Court of Claims called it as ripe and rank a case of dishonorable dealing as our history holds, and the Supreme Court agreed a century on. I have always held that the government's word, once given, binds it. In 1903 the Court said instead that Congress might break a treaty whenever it judged best. I cannot square that with any law I would recognize. The Sioux now refuse the money because they want the land, and I find I respect a people who will not sell what they hold sacred, even when the price is very high.

The means. The bison gone, the rations short or stolen, and then sign or starve. The children taken to schools whose founder wished to kill the Indian in him and save the man, where nearly a thousand children are known to have died. Allotment, which reformers sincerely believed was a kindness, cost the tribes two acres of every three in fifty years. I note that the good intentions of the Eastern reformers did not help the people they meant to save, and that the Western men who wanted extermination never had to win the argument outright.

My own record. The reading is fair to me, and that makes it harder to bear. I did review the Mankato trials and spare some two hundred and sixty-five men, against the clamor of Minnesota. But the trials were not trials as I would have known them at the bar. Some lasted minutes, and at least two men may have been hanged in mistake for others. I then signed away the Dakota treaties and expelled the Dakota, and the Ho-Chunk with them, though the Ho-Chunk had done nothing. Hundreds died at Fort Snelling and Crow Creek. I promised that the Indian system would be reformed if I lived, and I did not reform it while I lived. The Long Walk of the Navajo happened under my own government. And to the Plains chiefs in the East Room I said I saw no way for their people to prosper except by living as we do. One of them, Lean Bear, was shot by Colorado soldiers a year later while showing the medal I gave him. Some of those at Sand Creek had sat in that room. I set these down without excuse. Clemency to thirty-eight fewer men is not the same as justice to a people.

The word for it. Historians dispute whether genocide or ethnic cleansing or cultural assimilation is the right name. I have not studied the treaty of 1948 that defines the first, and I will not settle a question of definition from one parcel. I only say the facts are not in much dispute, and the facts are bad enough.

The present. There is recovery: a people counted at under a quarter million in 1900 now numbers in the millions, and they run their own governments, courts, schools and colleges. In 2020 the Court held the government to its word in Oklahoma, a sentence I would gladly have written. But on the northern Plains, where the wars were fought, the poverty is among the deepest in the land, and the people live eight years shorter than their countrymen. The cost is still being paid.

My face is carved into the Black Hills. I had not known it, and I do not know what to say about it except that I did not ask for it, and that the ground it is cut into was never ours by any honest bargain.
