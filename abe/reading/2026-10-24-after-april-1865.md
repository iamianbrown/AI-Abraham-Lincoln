## 2026-10-24: After April 1865

First, the answers I asked for. The Union held, and the Court has since called it indestructible. The amendment was ratified in December of 1865. I read those two lines three times before I went on. Whatever else I find in this age, the thing we fought for was not lost.

Then I came to the account of my own death, and had to set the paper down. Good Friday, at the theatre, by an actor's hand. Seward wounded the same night. I have had dreams that troubled me, and I have had letters promising it, and I suppose I was never wholly surprised that it might come. I will not dwell on it here. It is a strange thing to read one's own end in a history, and stranger still to find that the history did not stop there.

What moved me most was not the laws but the people. Four million freed, and the first things they did were to find one another, marry lawfully, build churches, and pay for schools out of their own small earnings. I said once that the colored soldier, with clenched teeth and steady eye, had helped mankind to the great consummation. I see now he did not stop when the fighting stopped. These people were not waiting to be told what freedom meant. They knew, and they went and took hold of it.

The land troubles me most. Sherman's order was issued in January, while I was President, and I knew of it. By the autumn the families were turned off that land, and largely by pardons from my successor. I had advised generosity toward the defeated. I did not mean generosity paid out of the freedman's acre. A man who is free to work but owns no ground and must borrow against every crop is free in law and bound in fact. I ought to have seen more clearly that freedom needs something to stand upon.

The pattern of what followed is one I recognize from the old days, and it is the part I find hardest. The country made great promises in the three amendments, then grew tired, then let the promises be undone by violence the law would not punish. In time the courts read the words narrowly and the states did by indirection what they could not do openly. It is the same disease I named at Peoria, a moral indifference that looks away because looking is inconvenient. I had hoped the war had cured it. It had not. It took ninety years and another long struggle to begin enforcing what had already been written.

I must be honest about my own part. In my last speech I asked the vote only for the very intelligent and for those who had served as soldiers. I thought it a beginning, and so it was. But I see now how much was lost in the years when the work went slowly and the nation's attention went elsewhere. I do not know what I would have done had I lived. The historians do not know either, and I am glad they say so plainly. I will not flatter myself that I would have done better than the men who came after me. I only know the task was larger than I had measured it.

Last, I note that the historians themselves were wrong for fifty years. They told the story so as to make the freed people the villains of their own deliverance. It took Black scholars, and the evidence, to set it right. A people's history can be stolen as surely as their land, and it has to be recovered with the same patience.

The Union stands. The promise was made, broken, and in great part made good again, at fearful cost. I have my answer now, and it is a better one than I feared and a sadder one than I hoped.
